# General Fitness & Health: Evidence-Graded Research Findings

**Scope:** The most scientifically defensible approach to improving *general fitness and health* — the goal profile of a person who wants to feel better, move better, and improve baseline health markers. This is explicitly **not** a hypertrophy, 1RM-maximization, or fat-loss-optimization review. Where the general-health prescription diverges from a performance prescription, that divergence is called out.

**Date of review:** August 2026
**Method:** Standalone literature review of major health-body guidelines (WHO 2020, ACSM 2018/2022, AHA, 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines Advisory Committee Scientific Report), Cochrane reviews, and dose-response meta-analyses, supplemented by individual RCTs where guideline-level evidence is absent.

### Confidence grading key

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **High** | Multiple meta-analyses / systematic reviews and/or concordant major-body guidelines (ACSM, WHO, AHA, Cochrane). Numbers are stable across sources. |
| **Moderate** | A meaningful body of RCTs or large cohorts, but with heterogeneity, mixed results, or reliance on observational data for the key number. |
| **Low** | Mechanistic reasoning, expert consensus, small/single trials, or extrapolation from adjacent populations. Directionally useful, numerically soft. |

A recurring caveat throughout: **effect sizes are far larger at the low end of the activity spectrum than at the high end.** For a general-health goal, moving someone from 0 to 60 min/week matters more than any programming refinement discussed below.

## 1. Cardiorespiratory Exercise: Dose and Dose-Response

### 1.1 The guideline minimum (Confidence: **High**)

Concordant across WHO 2020, the 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines (2nd ed.), ACSM, and AHA:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Moderate-intensity aerobic activity | **150–300 min/week** |
| Vigorous-intensity aerobic activity | **75–150 min/week** |
| Equivalent combination | 1 min vigorous ≈ 2 min moderate |
| Expressed in MET-minutes | **500–1,000 MET-min/week** (≈ 8.3–16.7 MET-hours/week) |
| Session distribution | Spread over **≥3 days/week**; no minimum bout length (the 10-minute-bout rule was **removed** in the 2018 guidelines) |
| Sedentary time | Replace sitting with activity of any intensity; no numeric threshold given, though cohorts suggest risk inflects above **~8–9.5 h/day** of sedentary time |

Intensity definitions (Confidence: **High**):
- **Moderate** = 3.0–5.9 METs ≈ **40–59% heart rate reserve (HRR)** or **64–76% HRmax**; RPE **12–13/20** (Borg) or **4–6/10**; "talk test" — can talk in sentences but not sing.
- **Vigorous** = ≥6.0 METs ≈ **60–89% HRR** or **77–95% HRmax**; RPE **14–17/20** or **7–8/10**; can speak only a few words at a time.
- **Light** = 1.6–2.9 METs. Counts toward total volume in the "any movement" sense but does not count toward the 150-min target.

**Practical anchor:** brisk walking at 3.0–4.0 mph ≈ 3.3–5.0 METs. 150 min/week of brisk walking ≈ 500–750 MET-min/week — i.e., the guideline floor is achievable entirely with walking.

*Source type: major-body guidelines synthesizing >1,000 studies (PAGAC 2018 Scientific Report).*

### 1.2 Dose-response curve for mortality (Confidence: **High** for shape, **Moderate** for exact inflection points)

The curve is **curvilinear with steeply diminishing returns**, not linear:

| Weekly MVPA volume | Approx. all-cause mortality risk reduction vs. inactive |
|---|---|
| 0 min/week | Reference (0%) |
| ~1 h/week (≈ 8 MET-h/month) | **~15–20%** |
| 150 min/week moderate (≈ 500 MET-min) | **~20–25%** |
| 300 min/week moderate (≈ 1,000 MET-min) | **~26–31%** |
| 450–600 min/week moderate | **~35–39%** (near-plateau) |
| >600 min/week (4× guideline) | **~35–40%**; no further meaningful gain, no clear harm |

Key numeric findings:
- **~50% of the total achievable mortality benefit is captured by the first ~half of the guideline minimum** (~75 min/week moderate). This is the single most important dose-response fact for a general-health goal.
- The curve **flattens between 300 and 600 min/week** of moderate activity. Lee et al. (2022, *Circulation*, ~116,000 adults, 30 y follow-up) found the lowest mortality at **150–300 min/week vigorous or 300–600 min/week moderate**, with no additional benefit beyond and — importantly — **no excess mortality** even at 4× the guideline.
- CVD mortality specifically: **30–40% risk reduction** at guideline-level volumes.
- Type 2 diabetes incidence: **25–35% reduction** at 150–300 min/week moderate.
- **Upper bound / "U-shape" question — genuinely contested.** Some cohorts (e.g., the Copenhagen City Heart Study on jogging) reported a J-curve with attenuated benefit at very high volumes; the largest and best-adjusted cohorts (Lee 2022; Arem 2015, ~661,000 adults) find a **plateau, not a reversal**. Expert opinion ranges from "no upper limit of benefit" (majority position, and the position supported by fitness-based rather than activity-based studies) to "possible small attenuation above ~10× guideline in a subset" (minority). For anyone in the general-fitness goal category this debate is **practically irrelevant** — it concerns volumes above ~10 h/week of vigorous training.

*Source type: dose-response meta-analyses of prospective cohorts (Arem 2015; Ekelund 2019 harmonized meta-analysis; Lee 2022) + PAGAC 2018. Observational, so residual confounding and reverse causation are real limits — hence Moderate on exact inflection points.*

### 1.3 Steps as an alternative currency (Confidence: **High**)

Useful because it is passively measurable and requires no intensity judgment.

| Population | Mortality risk plateaus at | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Adults ≥60 y | **6,000–8,000 steps/day** | No added mortality benefit beyond |
| Adults <60 y | **8,000–10,000 steps/day** | Plateau slightly higher |
| All adults, vs. ~2,700/day baseline | **~40–53% lower mortality** in highest vs. lowest quartile | Paluch 2022, Lancet Public Health, 15 cohorts, n=47,471 |

Steps **cadence** matters less than total volume; adjusting for total steps, higher cadence (>100 steps/min ≈ moderate intensity) adds only a small independent benefit. The 10,000-step figure has no scientific origin (it came from a 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing name) but is not far from the empirical plateau for under-60s.

*Source type: dose-response meta-analysis of accelerometer cohorts.*

### 1.4 What to actually prescribe (synthesis)

- **Floor for meaningful health benefit:** 60–75 min/week MVPA. Confidence: High.
- **Target:** 150–300 min/week moderate **or** 75–150 min/week vigorous **or** any equivalent mix. Confidence: High.
- **Optimal for mortality:** ~300–450 min/week moderate-equivalent. Confidence: Moderate.
- **Structure:** 3–5 sessions/week × 20–60 min, OR distributed accumulation (see §7). Both work; adherence should decide. Confidence: High.
- Including **1–2 sessions/week of vigorous or interval work** within the total is the highest-leverage single addition for VO2max (see §3). Confidence: High.

## 2. Resistance Training for General Health

### 2.1 Guideline minimum (Confidence: **High**)

WHO 2020 / ACSM / 2018 PAG all specify:

| Parameter | General-health value |
|---|---|
| Frequency | **≥2 days/week** (non-consecutive) |
| Coverage | **All major muscle groups** (legs, hips, back, chest, abdomen, shoulders, arms) — typically 8–10 exercises |
| Sets | **1–3 sets per exercise** |
| Reps | **8–12** (ACSM classic); **8–15** is fine; **10–15** specifically recommended for older/deconditioned adults |
| Load | **60–70% 1RM** for novice/intermediate; **≥50% 1RM** produces meaningful benefit in older adults |
| Proximity to failure | **2–4 reps in reserve (RIR)** is sufficient; training to failure is **not required** for health outcomes |
| Rest between sets | **60–120 s** (not critical for this goal) |
| Session duration | **20–40 min** is fully adequate |

### 2.2 The mortality dose-response for resistance training (Confidence: **Moderate**)

Momma et al. 2022 (*BJSM*, systematic review + meta-analysis, 16 cohorts):
- Muscle-strengthening activity independently associated with **10–17% lower risk** of all-cause mortality, CVD, total cancer, diabetes, lung cancer.
- **J-shaped curve with maximum benefit at ~30–60 min/week** of muscle-strengthening activity. Benefit **attenuates above ~130–140 min/week** (the observational signal weakens; this is likely confounding, not harm, but it strongly supports a low-volume prescription for a health goal).
- Diabetes was the exception: **L-shaped**, with benefit accumulating up to ~60 min/week then plateauing.
- **Combined** aerobic + resistance is markedly better than either alone: **~40% lower all-cause mortality, ~46% lower CVD mortality, ~28% lower cancer mortality** vs. neither.

**This is the headline number for this goal category: 30–60 minutes per week of resistance training — roughly two 20–30 minute sessions — sits at the peak of the observed mortality benefit curve.**

*Source type: meta-analysis of prospective cohorts; self-reported exposure, so Moderate.*

### 2.3 Minimum effective dose (Confidence: **Moderate–High**)

- **Strength:** Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020 (*Sports Medicine*, systematic review + meta-analysis) — **a single set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 2–3×/week for 8–12 weeks** produced significant (if suboptimal) 1RM increases in trained men (≈+17.5 kg squat, +8.3 kg bench). For untrained people the minimum is lower still.
- **Hypertrophy (relevant only as a floor):** meaningful growth appears from **~4 hard sets/muscle/week**, with dose-response continuing to ~12–20+ sets/muscle/week. For a general-health goal, the **4–10 sets/muscle/week** band captures nearly all the health-relevant adaptation.
- **Rep-range tolerance:** hypertrophy and health outcomes are largely **equivalent anywhere from ~5 to ~30+ reps** provided sets are taken close enough to failure (Schoenfeld et al. meta-analyses). **Strength** is more load-specific (favoring ≤8 reps at ≥80% 1RM). For general health, this means **rep range is a free parameter** — pick what's joint-friendly and enjoyable. Confidence: High.
- **Bone mineral density** is the one outcome that is *not* rep-range-agnostic. BMD responds to **high strain magnitude and high strain rate**: loads **≥80–85% 1RM** and/or **impact loading (jumping, hopping, bounding: 10–50 impacts/session, 3–5 sessions/week, ground reaction forces >2–3× body weight)**. The LIFTMOR trial (Watson et al. 2018, postmenopausal women with low BMD) used **8 months, 2×/week, 30 min, 5 sets × 5 reps at >85% 1RM (deadlift, overhead press, back squat) + jumping chin-ups**, producing **+2.9% lumbar spine and +0.3% femoral neck BMD** vs. losses in controls. Confidence: Moderate–High for the loading principle, Moderate for the exact protocol.

### 2.4 How the general-health prescription differs from a hypertrophy prescription

| Parameter | General health | Hypertrophy-optimized | Delta |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sessions/week | 2 (3 optional) | 3–6 | Lower |
| Sets/muscle/week | **4–10** | **12–20+** | ~half or less |
| Total weekly RT time | **30–60 min** | 150–300+ min | ~4–5× less |
| Rep range | **6–20+, freely chosen** | 6–12 emphasis (with 5–30 usable) | Much wider tolerance |
| Proximity to failure | **2–4 RIR** | 0–2 RIR on most sets | Less demanding |
| Load precision | ±10–15% is fine | Tracked, progressive | Much lower precision need |
| Progression | Add reps/load when RIR drifts to 4+ | Systematic weekly/biweekly | Less formal |
| Exercise selection | Compound, ~6–8 movements covering all groups | Per-muscle isolation coverage, angle variety | Simpler |
| Rest periods | 60–120 s, untimed | 2–3 min for compounds | Not a variable |
| Deloads/periodization | Rarely necessary | Programmed | Not needed |
| Failure/failure-adjacent training | Optional | Frequently used | Optional |

**Key insight for this goal:** essentially every parameter that requires precision in a hypertrophy program is a **wide, forgiving band** in a general-health program. The only parameters that genuinely matter are (a) it happens ≥2×/week, (b) it covers all major muscle groups, (c) effort is sufficient (RIR ≤4), and (d) it continues for years. Confidence: **High** for this framing.

### 2.5 Specific health outcomes and their doses

| Outcome | Dose | Expected effect | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sarcopenia prevention/reversal | 2–3×/week, 2–3 sets × 8–12 reps, 60–80% 1RM, ≥12 weeks | **+1.1 kg lean mass**, **+25–30% strength** in older adults over 12–24 weeks | High |
| Insulin sensitivity / HbA1c | 2–3×/week, ≥8 weeks | **HbA1c −0.3 to −0.6 percentage points** in T2D | High |
| Resting BP | 2–3×/week dynamic RT | **−4.6/−3.0 mmHg** | High |
| Bone density (lumbar/femoral neck) | 2×/week, ≥80% 1RM + impact, ≥8 months | **+1–3% BMD** vs. −1%/yr decline | Moderate |
| Functional strength (sit-to-stand, gait speed) | 2×/week, 12 weeks | 30-s chair-stand **+2–4 reps**; gait speed **+0.05–0.10 m/s** | High |
| Low back pain reduction | 2×/week trunk/hip strengthening | ~30–40% reduction in recurrence | Moderate |

## 3. VO2max and Cardiorespiratory Fitness

### 3.1 VO2max ↔ mortality (Confidence: **High**)

This is one of the strongest and best-replicated relationships in all of preventive medicine.

- **Each 1-MET (3.5 mL/kg/min) increase in CRF is associated with a 10–15% reduction in all-cause mortality** (pooled across cohorts; Kodama 2009 meta-analysis found **13% all-cause, 15% CHD/CVD** per MET). Confidence: **High**.
- Mandsager et al. 2018 (*JAMA Network Open*, n=122,007 treadmill tests, 8.4 y follow-up):
  - **Low fitness (<25th percentile) vs. elite (>97.7th percentile): adjusted HR 5.04** for all-cause mortality — i.e., being unfit carried a risk comparable in magnitude to, or exceeding, smoking, diabetes, or ESRD in that cohort.
  - **Elite vs. merely "high" fitness: HR 0.77** — benefit continued at the very top.
  - **No observed upper limit of benefit.** This is important: unlike self-reported *activity* volume (where a J-curve is debated), measured *fitness* shows monotonic benefit.
- The largest absolute gains come from moving out of the bottom quintile: **low → below-average fitness ≈ 50% mortality risk reduction**, larger than any subsequent step.
- CRF is a **stronger mortality predictor than smoking status, hypertension, or BMI** in most cohorts. Confidence: High.

### 3.2 Expected VO2max improvement by training status (Confidence: **High** for magnitudes, **Moderate** for exact timelines)

| Population | Program | Expected ΔVO2max | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sedentary/untrained | 3–5×/week, 20–60 min, moderate-vigorous | **+15–20%** (≈ +4–7 mL/kg/min) | 8–12 weeks |
| Sedentary, HIIT protocol | 3×/week interval | **+4.9 mL/kg/min** (pooled, Milanović 2015) vs. **+1.9** for MICT at matched duration | 8–12 weeks |
| Moderately trained | 3–5×/week structured | **+5–10%** | 12–24 weeks |
| Well-trained | High-volume + polarized | **+2–5%** or maintenance only | 6–12 months |
| Detraining (any) | Cessation | **−6–10% in 2–4 weeks**; ~50% of gains lost by 4–8 weeks; near-baseline by 12 weeks | — |
| Untrained, first 4 weeks | — | Detectable **+3–6%** and large *submaximal* improvements (lower HR at same pace) — usually noticed before VO2max plateau logic applies | 3–4 weeks |

**Age-related decline:** VO2max declines ~**10% per decade** after age 30 in sedentary adults (~0.4–0.5 mL/kg/min/year), and this **accelerates to ~15%/decade after 70**. Continued training roughly **halves the rate of decline** (~5%/decade). A 50-year-old who trains for 6 months can plausibly recover **10–15 years' worth of age-related VO2max decline**. Confidence: **Moderate–High**.

### 3.3 Protocols that raise VO2max (Confidence: **High** for the existence of effect; **Moderate** for head-to-head superiority)

| Protocol | Prescription | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Norwegian 4×4** | 4 × 4 min at **85–95% HRmax**, 3 min active recovery at 60–70%, 2–3×/week | Best-evidenced VO2max protocol; **+5–7 mL/kg/min in 8–12 weeks** in sedentary/cardiac populations |
| **10–20–30** | 5 min blocks of (30 s low, 20 s moderate, 10 s near-max) × 5, 3–4 blocks, 2–3×/week | Very time-efficient; +4% VO2max in 7 weeks in trained runners |
| **Short intervals** | 30 s–1 min at ~100–120% vVO2max, 1:1 recovery, 8–15 reps | Equivalent to 4×4 in most comparisons |
| **SIT (sprint intervals)** | 3 × 20 s all-out, 2 min recovery, 3×/week (~10 min total incl. warm-up) | **+12–19% VO2max in 6–12 weeks** in sedentary adults; effective but high perceived exertion → adherence risk |
| **MICT** | 30–60 min at 60–75% HRmax, 3–5×/week | Lower per-session stimulus, but scalable volume; excellent adherence and lowest injury risk |
| **Polarized 80/20** | ~80% of sessions easy (<VT1), ~20% hard (>VT2) | Best long-term structure; relevant once weekly volume exceeds ~3–4 h |

**Contested point:** whether HIIT is genuinely superior to MICT *when total work is equated* is debated. Meta-analyses consistently favor HIIT for VO2max per unit *time* (roughly **2× the improvement for ~40–60% of the time commitment**), but the advantage shrinks or disappears when energy expenditure is matched, and **HIIT shows lower long-term adherence in free-living populations** (dropout ~15–20% vs. ~10% for MICT in some trials). Expert opinion ranges from "HIIT is the efficient choice for time-limited adults" (ACSM-adjacent majority) to "HIIT's real-world advantage is largely an artifact of supervised short trials" (behavioral-science minority). **Defensible synthesis for a general-health goal: 1–2 vigorous/interval sessions per week embedded in a mostly-moderate base.** Confidence: **Moderate**.

## 4. Concurrent Training (Cardio + Resistance)

### 4.1 The interference effect — magnitude and relevance (Confidence: **Moderate–High**)

Meta-analyses (Wilson et al. 2012; Schumann et al. 2022; Petré et al. 2021):
- Concurrent training attenuates **explosive/power** development most (**effect size ≈ −0.31**), **maximal strength** modestly (**ES ≈ −0.18 to −0.20**), and **hypertrophy** minimally to not at all (**ES ≈ −0.09, often non-significant**).
- **Aerobic adaptations are essentially never impaired** by adding resistance training — interference is unidirectional.
- Interference scales with aerobic **volume and frequency**: it becomes detectable above roughly **>3 aerobic sessions/week and >20–30 min/session**, and is largest for **running** (eccentric loading, muscle damage) vs. **cycling** (minimal interference) — this modality difference is one of the more robust sub-findings.
- **Why it barely matters for this goal:** the interference effect is measured against *maximal* strength/power outcomes. For general health — where the resistance target is 30–60 min/week at 2–4 RIR and the goal is metabolic/bone/functional adaptation, not a competitive 1RM — a 5–10% attenuation of peak strength gain has **no meaningful health consequence**. Meanwhile the *combination* delivers ~40% lower all-cause mortality vs. ~15–25% for either alone (§2.2). **The combination is unambiguously correct for this goal.** Confidence: **High** for this conclusion.

### 4.2 Sequencing and spacing (Confidence: **Moderate**)

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Separate days | **Ideal.** Zero interference concern. |
| Same day, separated | **≥6 h between sessions** minimizes acute interference (≥3 h is the commonly cited minimum; ≥6–24 h is better) |
| Same session, general-health goal | **Order is largely irrelevant** — do the one you care most about first, or the one you'll skip if tired |
| Same session, mild strength priority | **Resistance first**, then cardio |
| Same session, mild endurance priority | **Cardio first** |
| High-intensity intervals + heavy lower-body lifting | Separate by **≥24 h** if both are hard |
| Cardio modality when lifting hard | **Cycling/rowing/elliptical** interfere less than running for lower-body strength |

### 4.3 Realistic weekly templates (Confidence: **Moderate**, extrapolated from guidelines + adherence literature)

| Time budget | Template |
|---|---|
| **~90 min/week (floor)** | 2 × 30 min full-body resistance + 1 × 25–30 min brisk walk/interval; plus daily steps |
| **~150 min/week (recommended)** | 2 × 35 min resistance + 2 × 30 min moderate cardio + 1 × 20 min vigorous/interval |
| **~200–240 min/week (optimal)** | 3 × 40 min resistance + 2 × 30 min moderate + 1 × 25 min vigorous + 10 min/day mobility |
| **Absolute minimum "still worth it"** | 2 × 20 min resistance + 7,000 steps/day |

**Adherence-based session count:** dropout rises materially above **4–5 planned sessions/week** in general-population trials; **3–4 sessions/week is the sweet spot** for 12-month adherence (typically 50–65% retention vs. 30–45% for 5–6×/week programs). Confidence: **Moderate**. Session duration above ~60 min is a stronger dropout predictor than session frequency. Confidence: **Low–Moderate**.

## 5. Mobility, Flexibility, and Balance

### 5.1 Flexibility/mobility (Confidence: **High** for the dose, **Low–Moderate** for health outcomes)

ACSM prescription:
- **2–3 days/week minimum; daily is more effective**
- **Static holds: 10–30 s** per stretch (**30–60 s for adults >65 y**)
- **2–4 repetitions** per muscle group, accumulating **~60 s total per muscle group per session**
- **~10 min/session** covering major muscle-tendon units
- Stretch when muscles are warm (post-exercise or after 5–10 min warm-up)
- **Dynamic mobility 5–10 min pre-session**; static stretching pre-session transiently reduces force output (**−2% to −5%** for holds >60 s) — practically negligible below 60 s, but the convention of static-after / dynamic-before is well founded

**Honest caveat:** ROM improves reliably (**+5–20% joint ROM in 4–8 weeks**), but evidence that flexibility work independently reduces injury or improves general health outcomes is **weak** (Confidence: **Low**). Cochrane and military-cohort reviews found stretching does **not** meaningfully reduce injury incidence; **warm-up and strength training do**. Mobility work is justified for *function, comfort, and movement quality*, not for injury prevention or longevity. **Do not let mobility work displace cardio or resistance dose** — it has an order of magnitude less evidence behind it for health outcomes.

Note: **resistance training through full ROM produces flexibility gains equal to static stretching** (multiple RCTs). A well-executed full-ROM lifting program can substitute for a substantial portion of dedicated stretching. Confidence: **Moderate**.

### 5.2 Balance training (Confidence: **High**)

This is where age gating is sharpest and effect sizes are largest.

Sherrington et al. 2019 (**Cochrane**, 108 RCTs, n=23,407, community-dwelling ≥60 y):
- Exercise overall reduces **rate of falls by 23%** (RaR 0.77) and **number of fallers by 15%**.
- **Balance + functional exercise** programs: **rate of falls −24%** (high-certainty evidence).
- **Balance + functional + resistance** (multi-component): **rate of falls −34%** — the best-performing category.
- **Tai Chi: rate of falls −19%.**
- Resistance training alone or walking alone: **no significant fall reduction** — balance-specific challenge is the active ingredient.

**Dose (Confidence: Moderate–High):**
- **≥3 hours/week of balance/functional training** is the threshold above which effects are consistently larger (Sherrington dose-response analysis).
- **≥50 hours cumulative** is the frequently cited threshold for robust fall reduction (~6 months at 2 h/week).
- Practically: **3 sessions/week × 20–30 min**, or **10–15 min/day**.
- Programs must be **progressively challenging**: reduce base of support, remove hand support, add head turns/dual tasks, use unstable or compliant surfaces, close eyes. Balance training done at a comfortable level produces little benefit.
- **Otago Exercise Programme** (well-validated): 5 strength + 12 balance exercises, **3×/week, 30 min**, plus walking 2–3×/week; reduces falls **~35%** in those ≥80 y or with prior falls.

**WHO 2020 explicitly adds for adults ≥65 y:** multicomponent physical activity emphasizing **functional balance and strength training at moderate or greater intensity, ≥3 days/week**, in addition to the standard aerobic and muscle-strengthening recommendations. Confidence: **High**.

**Age gating (Confidence: Moderate):**

| Age | Balance training priority | Suggested dose |
|---|---|---|
| <40 | Low (integrate incidentally: single-leg work in lifting) | 5 min/week incidental |
| 40–59 | Low–moderate; cheap insurance | 5–10 min, 2×/week |
| 60–69 | **Moderate–high; formal inclusion warranted** | 10–20 min, 3×/week |
| 70–79 | **High** | 20–30 min, 3×/week |
| 80+ | **Highest — co-equal with resistance training** | 30 min, 3×/week (Otago-style), supervised initially |

The inflection is around **age 60–65**; ~28–35% of adults ≥65 fall each year, rising to ~50% at ≥80.

### 5.3 Integration without displacing the main dose (Confidence: **Low–Moderate**, practical synthesis)

- Fold **5–10 min dynamic mobility** into the warm-up (counts as light activity, displaces nothing).
- Fold **balance work into resistance sessions** as the warm-up or between sets: single-leg RDL, split squats, step-ups, tandem stance, farmer's carries — these are simultaneously resistance and balance stimuli.
- Do **static stretching post-session or on rest days** (10 min).
- Total added time cost: **~15 min/session**, no reduction in cardio/resistance minutes.

## 6. Resting HR, HR Recovery, and Blood Pressure

### 6.1 Resting heart rate (Confidence: **Moderate–High**)

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Typical untrained adult RHR | **60–80 bpm** |
| Trained recreational | **50–65 bpm** |
| Expected reduction from 8–12 weeks of endurance training | **−3 to −7 bpm** (meta-analytic mean ≈ **−4 to −5 bpm**) |
| Longer-term (6–12 months) | **−5 to −10 bpm** cumulative |
| Detectable trend emerges | **3–4 weeks** (needs multi-week averaging; day-to-day noise is ±3–5 bpm) |
| Prognostic value | Each **+10 bpm RHR** ≈ **+9% all-cause mortality, +8% CV mortality** (dose-response meta-analyses) |
| RHR >80–90 bpm | Independently associated with elevated risk |

**Caveats for use as a tracked marker:** RHR is confounded by alcohol (+3–10 bpm), poor sleep (+2–8 bpm), illness (+5–15 bpm), caffeine, dehydration, ambient temperature, and stress. It is a **good weekly/monthly trend metric and a poor daily metric.** Wrist-wearable overnight-minimum RHR is reasonably valid (±2–3 bpm vs. ECG). Confidence: **Moderate**.

### 6.2 Heart rate recovery (Confidence: **Moderate–High**)

- **HRR1 (drop in bpm 1 min after stopping exercise):** the canonical abnormal threshold is **≤12 bpm** after a cool-down (Cole et al. 1999, *NEJM*), which carried a **relative risk ~4** for 6-year mortality. With abrupt cessation (no cool-down) the abnormal threshold is **≤18 bpm**.
- **HRR2:** normal ≥**22 bpm** at 2 minutes.
- Typical healthy values: **HRR1 = 15–25 bpm**; well-trained: **25–40 bpm**.
- **Training effect: +5 to +10 bpm improvement in HRR1 over 8–12 weeks** of aerobic training (cardiac rehab trials show +7 to +12 bpm over 6–12 weeks in patients). Confidence: **Moderate**.
- HRR is arguably the **best cheap non-lab proxy for autonomic/parasympathetic function** and responds faster than RHR.
- **Standardization is essential**: same test, same intensity, same posture, same cool-down protocol. Uncontrolled HRR readings are nearly meaningless.

### 6.3 Blood pressure (Confidence: **High**)

Edwards et al. 2023 (*BJSM*, network meta-analysis, **270 RCTs, n=15,827**) — reductions in resting BP by modality:

| Modality | Δ Systolic / Diastolic (mmHg) |
|---|---|
| **Isometric** (e.g., wall squat, handgrip) | **−8.24 / −4.00** |
| **Combined aerobic + resistance** | **−6.04 / −2.54** |
| **Dynamic resistance** | **−4.55 / −3.04** |
| **Aerobic** | **−4.49 / −2.53** |
| **HIIT** | **−4.08 / −2.50** |

Additional numbers:
- Effects are **~2× larger in hypertensive individuals** (aerobic training: **−8 to −9 / −5 to −6 mmHg** in hypertensives vs. −2 to −3 mmHg in normotensives). Confidence: **High**.
- **Timescale:** measurable within **4 weeks**; largely complete by **8–12 weeks**; plateaus thereafter.
- **Acute post-exercise hypotension:** **−5 to −8 mmHg systolic for 2–22 h** after a single session — one reason near-daily activity helps BP control.
- A **−5 mmHg systolic** population-level reduction corresponds to roughly **−10% stroke and −7% CHD** risk.
- **Isometric protocol** (best-evidenced): **4 × 2 min at 30–40% MVC (or wall squat at ~95–100 bpm HR), 1–4 min rest, 3×/week**. Confidence: **Moderate–High** — the network meta-analysis is strong, but isometric trials are smaller and shorter than aerobic trials, and this finding is newer than most guidelines, which still lead with aerobic. Guidelines have not yet been rewritten around it; treat isometric work as a **high-value adjunct, not a replacement**.

### 6.4 Value as trackable markers (Confidence: **Moderate**)

Ranked by signal-to-noise for a general-fitness user:

1. **Resting BP** — highest clinical validity, cheap, low noise if measured properly (seated, 5 min rest, 2–3 readings averaged, same time of day). Meaningful change: **≥5 mmHg sustained over 2+ weeks**.
2. **Submaximal HR at fixed workload** (e.g., HR at 5 km/h on a 3% incline, or 3-min step test) — **most sensitive early fitness marker**; improves **5–15 bpm in 4–8 weeks** in untrained people. Underrated.
3. **HRR1** — good, requires protocol standardization.
4. **Resting HR** — easiest to collect passively, most confounded. Use 7-day rolling averages.
5. **HRV (rMSSD/SDNN)** — highly individual, large day-to-day variance (CV ~10–20%); useful as a **personal 7-day-baseline deviation** signal, near-useless as a cross-person comparison. Confidence: **Low–Moderate**.

## 7. Minimum Effective Dose and "Exercise Snacking"

### 7.1 The bout-length question is settled (Confidence: **High**)

The 2018 US Physical Activity Guidelines **removed the requirement that activity occur in bouts of ≥10 minutes**. Accumulated activity of **any bout length** counts toward the weekly total, based on accelerometer cohort evidence showing equivalent mortality associations for sporadic vs. bouted MVPA.

### 7.2 VILPA — vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity (Confidence: **Moderate**)

Stamatakis et al. 2022 (*Nature Medicine*, UK Biobank accelerometry, n=25,241 **non-exercisers**, mean age 61.8 y, 6.9 y follow-up):
- **3 bouts/day of 1–2 min vigorous activity (~3–4 min/day total)** associated with:
  - **~38–40% lower all-cause mortality**
  - **~48–49% lower CVD mortality**
  - **~38–40% lower cancer mortality**
- Association was **near-linear** with steepest gains in the first few minutes/day.
- Companion analysis: VILPA associated with **~17–18% lower total cancer incidence** at ~3.5 min/day.
- Examples: fast/uphill walking for 1–2 min, stair climbing, carrying shopping, vigorous housework.

**Important limitations:** observational; the comparison group is *self-reported non-exercisers*, so residual confounding (frailty, subclinical disease) is a real concern; effect sizes are almost certainly inflated relative to what an RCT would show. Treat the *direction and accessibility* as robust; treat the *magnitude* skeptically.

### 7.3 Structured exercise snacking (Confidence: **Moderate**)

RCT-level evidence:
- **Stair-climbing snacks:** 3 × 20 s all-out stair climbing (~60 s of hard work), 3×/week, 6 weeks → **+5% VO2peak** in untrained adults (Jenkins/Gibala, McMaster).
- **Vigorous stair snacks, 3×/day, 3 days/week, 6 weeks** → **+5% peak power output** in sedentary adults.
- **Body-weight "exercise snacks" in older adults:** e.g., 5 × 1 min sit-to-stands, twice daily, 4 weeks → **+5–10% leg strength/power** vs. no change in control.
- **Postprandial walking:** **2–5 min of light walking within 60–90 min after a meal** significantly blunts glucose excursions (**−17% to −30% postprandial glucose AUC**; Buffey 2022 meta-analysis) — a distinctly larger metabolic effect than the same walking done at an arbitrary time. Confidence: **Moderate–High**.
- **Breaking up sitting:** **3 min of light walking or simple resistance moves every 30 min** reduces postprandial glucose ~**15–30%** and insulin ~**20–25%** vs. uninterrupted sitting. Confidence: **High** for acute metabolic effects, **Low** for whether it translates to hard outcomes.

### 7.4 Where snacking is and isn't sufficient

| Outcome | Snacking sufficient? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| All-cause/CV mortality association | **Likely yes**, especially for the previously sedentary | VILPA evidence; observational |
| Postprandial glucose / insulin sensitivity | **Yes — arguably superior** to one long session | Frequency beats duration here |
| Blood pressure | **Partially** | Acute post-exercise hypotension favors frequent bouts |
| VO2max | **Partially** (~+3–6%) | Structured intervals produce 2–4× more |
| Muscle strength/hypertrophy | **Partially in the untrained/older**; **no** in the trained | Needs progressive load eventually |
| Bone density | **No** | Requires high-magnitude loading or impact volume |
| Balance / fall reduction | **Yes, if the snack is balance-specific** | Progressive challenge is the requirement, not duration |
| Meaningful body-composition change | **No** | Insufficient energy expenditure |

**Synthesis:** exercise snacking is a **legitimate primary strategy for the previously sedentary and for people who will not adhere to structured sessions**, and a **valuable supplement for everyone else**. It is **not** a full substitute for structured resistance training or for the VO2max stimulus. Best-defensible position: **snacking as the floor and on-ramp; structured sessions as the ceiling.** Confidence: **Moderate**.

**Prescribable snack protocol:** 3–5 bouts/day × 1–3 min of vigorous effort (stairs, brisk uphill walk, 20–30 body-weight squats, carries), totaling **5–15 min/day**, plus **2–5 min of walking after each main meal**, plus **3 min of movement per 30–60 min of sitting**.

## 8. Sleep

### 8.1 Duration and consistency (Confidence: **High**)

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Duration, adults 18–64 | **7–9 h/night** |
| Duration, adults ≥65 | **7–8 h/night** |
| Lowest-mortality point | **~7 h/night** (U-shaped curve) |
| Short sleep (<7 h) mortality | **+12–14%** all-cause |
| Long sleep (≥9 h) mortality | **+30–38%** all-cause (largely reverse causation / illness) |
| Bed/wake time consistency | Within **±30–60 min** night to night, including weekends |
| Sleep efficiency target | **≥85%** (time asleep ÷ time in bed) |
| Sleep onset latency | **<30 min** |
| Wake after sleep onset | **<30 min** |
| "Social jetlag" threshold | **>1 h** weekday–weekend midpoint shift associated with worse cardiometabolic markers |

**Notably, sleep *regularity* has recently outperformed sleep *duration* as a mortality predictor** — Windred et al. 2023 (UK Biobank accelerometry, n≈60,000): the most regular sleepers had **20–48% lower all-cause mortality** than the most irregular, and regularity was a **stronger predictor than duration**. Confidence: **Moderate–High** (single large cohort, but well-conducted and consistent with adjacent chronobiology literature).

### 8.2 Sleep ↔ training adherence and recovery (general-health framing) (Confidence: **Moderate**)

Distinct from hypertrophy/strength-specific mechanisms (GH pulsatility, muscle protein synthesis, testosterone:cortisol), the general-fitness-relevant links are:

- **Injury risk:** adolescents sleeping <8 h/night were **1.7× more likely** to sustain injury (Milewski 2014). Adult/military cohorts show similar direction (**~1.5–2.0× risk** below ~6 h). Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **Adherence:** poor sleep quality predicts next-day exercise skipping; conversely, in the *reverse* direction the evidence is stronger — **exercise improves sleep**: meta-analyses show **+15–25 min total sleep time**, **−10 to −13 min sleep onset latency**, **+5–8 percentage points sleep efficiency**, and a **~0.5–0.7 SD improvement in PSQI** in adults with sleep complaints, after **8–12 weeks** of regular moderate exercise. Confidence: **High**.
- **Perceived exertion and mood:** sleep restriction to ~5 h raises RPE at fixed workloads by **~1–2 Borg points** and degrades mood/motivation, making a given program *feel* harder. Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **Timing myth partially debunked:** evening exercise does **not** generally impair sleep; meta-analyses find no harm and often small benefit, **except for vigorous exercise ending <1 h before bed**, which can delay onset by ~10–20 min in some individuals. Confidence: **Moderate**.

**Prescribable sleep parameters:** 7–9 h opportunity in bed; consistent wake time within ±30 min 7 days/week; last vigorous session ending **≥1 h** (ideally ≥2 h) before bed; caffeine cutoff **≥8 h** before bed (caffeine half-life 5–6 h); alcohol avoided within **3 h** of bed (reduces REM ~9–20% and worsens second-half sleep quality).

## 9. Nutrition for General Health

Scope note: dietary *patterns* and sufficiency ranges, not macro optimization for a body-composition goal.

### 9.1 Protein (Confidence: **High** for the ranges, **Moderate** for the upper end)

| Population | Daily protein |
|---|---|
| RDA (minimum to avoid deficiency, not optimum) | **0.8 g/kg/day** |
| Active adult, general health | **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** |
| Adults ≥65 (sarcopenia prevention; PROT-AGE / ESPEN consensus) | **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day minimum**, **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day** if training or ill |
| Hypertrophy-optimized (for contrast only) | **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day**; Morton 2018 meta-analysis found benefit plateauing at **~1.6 g/kg/day** (CI up to 2.2) |
| Per-meal distribution | **0.24 g/kg/meal** (young), **0.40 g/kg/meal** (older, due to anabolic resistance); **~20–40 g per meal across 3–4 meals** |
| Leucine threshold per meal | **~2.5–3 g** |

For a general-health goal, **1.2–1.6 g/kg/day** captures nearly all benefit; going higher is a hypertrophy-goal decision, not a health one. Note the difference is not trivially small for older adults — **the ≥65 minimum is 25–50% above the RDA**, and the RDA is a common shortfall in that group.

### 9.2 Dietary pattern (Confidence: **High**)

The strongest evidence is for *patterns*, not individual nutrients:

| Component | Target | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Fiber** | **25–30 g/day** (women ≥25 g, men ≥38 g per IOM); each **+8 g/day** ≈ **−7% all-cause mortality, −5–27% CVD/T2D/CRC incidence** | High (Reynolds 2019 Lancet, 185 cohorts + 58 RCTs) |
| **Fruit & vegetables** | **≥5 servings/day (~400 g)**; risk plateaus around **5 servings** (~800 g in some analyses); **−13% all-cause mortality** at 5 servings | High |
| **Whole grains** | **≥3 servings/day (~90 g)**; **−17–22% all-cause mortality** | High |
| **Legumes/nuts** | Nuts **~28 g/day** (≈ −20% all-cause mortality); legumes ~3–4 servings/week | Moderate–High |
| **Fish** | **1–2 servings/week** (~200–350 g); ~−10% CVD mortality | Moderate–High |
| **Sodium** | **<2,300 mg/day**; ideal **~1,500–2,000 mg/day** (WHO: <2,000 mg) | High for BP effect (−5/−3 mmHg from a 1,000 mg/day reduction in hypertensives) |
| **Added sugar** | **<10% of energy** (WHO), ideally **<5%**; AHA: **<25 g/day (women), <36 g/day (men)** | Moderate–High |
| **Ultra-processed food (NOVA 4)** | Lower is better; each **+10% of energy from UPF** ≈ **+3% all-cause mortality, +6–12% CVD risk** | Moderate (observational; confounded, but consistent) |
| **Alcohol** | No amount is protective on net; risk rises measurably above **~100 g/week (~7 standard drinks)** | Moderate–High (Mendelian randomization has largely eliminated the "J-curve") |
| **Saturated fat** | **<10% of energy**; replacing 5% of energy from SFA with PUFA ≈ **−9–13% CHD** | Moderate (contested at the margins) |

**Named patterns with outcome evidence:** Mediterranean diet (PREDIMED RCT: **−30% major CV events** over ~5 y, n=7,447), DASH (**−8 to −11 / −3 to −6 mmHg** systolic/diastolic), and healthy plant-based patterns all converge on the same components. **Adherence, not pattern selection, is the operative variable.** Confidence: **High**.

### 9.3 Hydration (Confidence: **Moderate**)

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Total water intake (all sources, incl. food ~20%) | **Women ~2.7 L/day; men ~3.7 L/day** (IOM Adequate Intake) |
| Beverages only | **~2.0–2.2 L/day (women), ~3.0 L/day (men)** |
| During exercise | **0.4–0.8 L/h**, adjusted for sweat rate, heat, body size |
| Post-exercise replacement | **1.25–1.5 L per kg of body mass lost** |
| Performance decrement threshold | **>2% body mass loss** begins to impair endurance performance and cognition |
| Practical marker | Urine color pale straw; first-morning urine specific gravity **<1.020** |

Caveat: "8 glasses a day" has no strong evidence base, and thirst is an adequate guide for most sedentary-to-moderately-active adults in temperate conditions. Confidence: **Moderate**. Older adults have blunted thirst and warrant a scheduled-intake approach. Confidence: **Moderate**.

## 10. Injury Prevention and Long-Term Sustainability

### 10.1 Progression rate (Confidence: **Low–Moderate** — weaker evidence than commonly assumed)

- The **"10% rule"** (increase weekly volume ≤10%) is **not well supported by RCTs** — the Buist 2008 trial of a graded 8-week vs. 13-week running program found **no difference in injury rate (~20% in both)**. It survives as a reasonable heuristic, not an evidence-based law. Confidence: **Low**.
- **Acute:Chronic Workload Ratio (ACWR):** the frequently cited "sweet spot" of **0.8–1.3** with elevated risk above **1.5** comes from team-sport cohorts and has been **substantially methodologically criticized** (Impellizzeri et al. 2020; spurious-correlation and mathematical-coupling critiques). Directionally useful — *large, abrupt jumps in load raise injury risk* — but the specific thresholds should not be treated as validated. Confidence: **Low**.
- **Defensible general-population progression rates:**
  - Aerobic volume: **+5–10% per week**, with a **non-progressing "consolidation" week every 3–4 weeks**. Confidence: Low–Moderate.
  - Running specifically: novices should cap at **+10% weekly mileage** and use **run/walk intervals for the first 4–8 weeks**; running-injury incidence in novices is **~17–33 per 1,000 hours** vs. **~8 per 1,000 hours** in recreational regulars — the first months are the high-risk period. Confidence: Moderate.
  - Resistance load: **+2–5% (upper body) / +5–10% (lower body)** when the target rep count is exceeded by 1–2 reps at the prescribed RIR ("double progression"). Confidence: Moderate.
  - **Return after ≥3 months layoff: start at 50–60% of previously tolerated volume** and rebuild over **4–6 weeks**. Confidence: Low (expert consensus).

### 10.2 What actually reduces injury (Confidence: **High**)

Lauersen et al. 2014/2018 (*BJSM*, meta-analysis of RCTs, n≈26,000):
- **Strength training reduced sports injuries by ~66% (RR 0.34) and overuse injuries by ~50%** — by far the most effective single intervention.
- **Proprioception/balance training: ~45% reduction.**
- **Stretching: no significant effect (RR ~0.96).**
- **Multi-component programs: ~35–40% reduction.**
- Dose-response: injury risk falls further with **greater strength-training volume, up to ~2–3 sessions/week**; benefit plateaus thereafter.

Neuromuscular warm-up programs (FIFA 11+, Knee Control style): **~30–50% reduction in lower-limb injuries**; dose is **15–20 min, ≥2×/week, sustained ≥3 months** (compliance-dependent — high-compliance groups get ~2× the benefit). Confidence: **High** in sport, **Moderate** extrapolated to general fitness.

### 10.3 Recovery parameters (Confidence: **Moderate**)

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Rest between hard sessions for the same muscle group | **48 h** (24 h adequate for light/moderate) |
| Recovery/deload frequency, general population | **1 lighter week every 6–12 weeks** (optional; less necessary at general-health volumes than at hypertrophy volumes) |
| DOMS onset/peak/resolution | 12–24 h / 24–72 h / 5–7 days |
| Acceptable soreness for a general goal | Mild, **resolving within 48 h**; persistent soreness signals excessive progression |
| Weekly hard-session cap (untrained/returning) | **≤2 genuinely hard sessions/week** in the first 8 weeks |
| Sleep (see §8) | 7–9 h; <6 h roughly **1.5–2×** injury risk |

### 10.4 Long-term adherence (Confidence: **Moderate**)

Empirical adherence findings relevant to decades-long sustainability:

- **~50% of exercise-program starters drop out within 6 months** (a remarkably stable finding across 40 years of literature).
- **Enjoyment/affective response during exercise predicts 6–12-month adherence better than intentions, knowledge, or perceived benefit.** People who feel better *during and immediately after* a session at a self-selected intensity adhere substantially longer. This argues for prescribing **self-selected or moderate intensity as the default** for the previously sedentary, with vigorous work added later. Confidence: **Moderate–High** (Ekkekakis' affective-response line of research).
- **Self-selected intensity** produces adherence advantages of roughly **10–20 percentage points** over imposed high intensity in sedentary populations.
- **Variety** (rotating 2–4 modalities) modestly improves adherence and reduces overuse loading on any one tissue. Confidence: **Low–Moderate**.
- **Habit formation** to automaticity plateau: median **~66 days** (range 18–254) for a simple daily behavior; exercise sits toward the slow end (~90+ days). Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **Behavioral techniques with the strongest effect sizes:** self-monitoring, specific action planning (when/where/what), and goal-setting; combining self-monitoring with ≥1 other self-regulatory technique yields the largest effects. Confidence: **High** (Michie et al. meta-analyses).
- **Missing sessions:** a single missed session has no measurable physiological consequence; the risk is behavioral. Detraining is negligible below **~1 week**; meaningful VO2max loss begins around **2 weeks** (−6–10%); strength is preserved for **~3 weeks** and preserved at **1/3 of normal volume** almost indefinitely. This "maintenance is cheap" fact (**1 session/week or 1/3 volume maintains strength for months**) is the strongest argument for a low, sustainable baseline over a high, brittle one. Confidence: **Moderate–High**.

## 11. Individual Differences

### 11.1 Sex (Confidence: **Moderate**; Low for the strongest claims)

**Where there is genuinely little reason to differ:**
- Relative strength gains from resistance training are **similar between sexes** (percentage gains equal or slightly higher in women for upper body from a lower base). Confidence: **High**.
- Guideline aerobic and resistance doses are **identical for men and women** in WHO, ACSM, AHA. Confidence: **High**.
- Rep ranges, progression models, exercise selection: **no sex-specific evidence justifying divergence**. Confidence: **Moderate**.

**Where sex-linked differences are real but mostly modest:**
| Difference | Numbers | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute VO2max | Women ~**15–30% lower** absolute; ~**10–15% lower** when scaled to fat-free mass (hemoglobin, heart size) | High |
| Fatigue resistance | Women generally **more fatigue-resistant** at matched relative intensity for isometric/submaximal tasks; may tolerate **higher reps / shorter rests** and recover faster between sets | Moderate |
| Recovery between sessions | Some evidence of **faster inter-session recovery** in women; effect small | Low–Moderate |
| ACL injury risk | Women **2–8× higher** ACL injury rate in pivoting sports → neuromuscular training (landing mechanics, hip/hamstring strength) has a higher expected value | High |
| Bone health | Women lose **~1–2%/year bone mass** in the ~5–7 years around menopause (up to 20% cumulative) → **loading-focused resistance + impact training becomes disproportionately valuable from ~age 45** | High |
| Iron status | Menstruating women have **~10–20% prevalence of iron deficiency**; relevant when fatigue/aerobic performance stall unexplainedly | Moderate |
| Menstrual-cycle-based periodization | **Evidence is weak and inconsistent.** A 2020 systematic review/meta-analysis (McNulty et al.) found performance may be *trivially* reduced in the early follicular phase, with very low quality of evidence. Individual symptom-based adjustment is defensible; rigid phase-based programming is **not** evidence-supported | Low |
| Menopause / HRT | Resistance + impact training partially offsets menopausal bone and lean-mass loss; magnitude of exercise benefit is not reduced | Moderate |

**Bottom line:** for a general-fitness goal, **program parameters should not be systematically split by sex.** The defensible sex-linked adjustments are (a) earlier and heavier emphasis on bone-loading work for women from ~45 onward, (b) higher priority on landing/knee neuromuscular work if pivoting sports are involved, and (c) awareness of iron status. Everything else is individual variation that dwarfs sex-average variation. Confidence: **Moderate**.

### 11.2 Training experience (Confidence: **Moderate**)

**Complete beginner (never trained, or >2 years off):**
| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Starting aerobic volume | **60–90 min/week** total, at light-to-moderate intensity; build to 150 min over **6–12 weeks** |
| Starting resistance | **2 sessions/week, 1–2 sets × 8–15 reps, 6–8 exercises**, RIR 3–5 |
| First progression | Add **reps before load**; hold load constant for the first **2–4 weeks** while movement quality consolidates |
| Expected strength gain | **+20–40% in 8–12 weeks** (predominantly neural for the first 4–6 weeks) |
| Expected VO2max gain | **+10–20% in 8–12 weeks** |
| Intensity | **Self-selected / RPE 3–5 of 10** for most work initially; adherence >> optimality |
| Time to habit stability | **~10–14 weeks** |
| Highest-risk window for dropout and injury | **Weeks 1–8** |

**Returning after a long layoff (previously trained, ≥3–6 months off):**
- **Muscle memory is real and quantified:** myonuclear retention and motor-pattern retention mean retraining reaches prior levels roughly **2–3× faster** than the original acquisition. Confidence: **Moderate**.
- **The specific hazard is that capacity returns faster than connective-tissue tolerance.** Tendon and ligament adaptation lags muscle adaptation by **weeks to months** (collagen turnover half-life is far longer than muscle protein). This is the mechanism behind returning-athlete injuries.
- **Prescription:** restart at **50–60% of the last-tolerated volume and ~60–70% of last-tolerated load**, progress ~**10%/week**, expect to reach prior baseline in **4–8 weeks** (vs. 6–12 months originally). Explicitly cap enthusiasm in weeks 1–3. Confidence: **Low–Moderate** (expert consensus, sparse direct trial data).

**Some baseline fitness, wants to "get healthier" rather than optimize a metric:**
- Already at or near the 150 min/week floor. The highest-yield additions, in order:
  1. **Add resistance training if absent** (the most common gap; ~30–35% of adults meet aerobic guidelines but only ~24–30% meet both).
  2. **Add 1–2 vigorous/interval sessions per week** (largest VO2max return per minute).
  3. **Add balance/loading work if ≥50.**
  4. **Increase non-exercise activity** (steps, sitting breaks) — often a larger total-energy and metabolic lever than adding a session.
- Expected further gains are smaller: **VO2max +5–10% over 12–24 weeks**, strength **+10–20%**. Framing progress around **process metrics and functional markers rather than fitness-test deltas** is more sustainable here. Confidence: **Moderate**.

### 11.3 Age

This is where the guidelines themselves change and where numbers matter most.

**WHO 2020 guideline changes by age (Confidence: High):**
- Adults 18–64: 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 vigorous + muscle-strengthening ≥2 d/week.
- Adults **≥65: identical aerobic and strengthening targets, PLUS multicomponent physical activity emphasizing functional balance and strength training at moderate+ intensity, ≥3 days/week**, to enhance functional capacity and prevent falls.
- Adults ≥65 unable to meet the aerobic target: **"be as physically active as abilities allow."** No floor is imposed.

#### Ages 40–59 (Confidence: Moderate–High)

| Item | Numbers |
|---|---|
| Muscle mass loss | **~0.5–1.0%/year after ~40**; strength loss faster at **~1.0–1.5%/year**; **power** faster still (~2–3%/year) |
| VO2max decline | **~10%/decade**; halved by training |
| BMD | Men lose ~0.5%/yr from ~50; women lose **1–2%/yr peri/post-menopause** |
| Prescription changes vs. 20s/30s | Essentially **none required** — the same 150–300 min + 2×/week resistance applies. Add: (a) at least **some heavy loading (≥80% 1RM) or impact work 1–2×/week** for bone, (b) **power/explosive work** (fast concentric intent, 30–60% 1RM, 3–6 reps, 2–3 sets) begins to earn its place, (c) **5–10 min of balance work weekly** as cheap insurance |
| Realistic expectation | Adaptation rate is only modestly reduced vs. 20s; recovery need is somewhat higher (**48–72 h** between hard sessions for the same pattern) |

#### Ages 60–79 (Confidence: High)

| Item | Numbers |
|---|---|
| Aerobic | Same 150–300 min/week moderate; walking-based is sufficient. **6,000–8,000 steps/day** is the mortality plateau |
| Resistance | **2–3×/week**, **1–3 sets × 10–15 reps at 50–80% 1RM** (start at the lower end); progressive |
| **Power training** | **2×/week, 3 sets × 6–10 reps at 40–60% 1RM with maximal concentric velocity** — power correlates with function (stair climbing, fall avoidance) **more strongly than strength** |
| **Balance** | **≥3 days/week**, 20–30 min, progressively challenging; **≥50 h cumulative** for robust fall reduction |
| Flexibility | 2–3×/week, static holds **30–60 s** (longer than for younger adults) |
| Protein | **1.0–1.2 g/kg/day minimum**, **~0.4 g/kg per meal** to overcome anabolic resistance |
| Expected gains | Strength **+25–30% in 12 weeks**; lean mass **+1.0–1.1 kg**; gait speed **+0.05–0.10 m/s**; 30-s chair-stand **+2–4 reps** |
| Fall statistics | ~**28–35%** of ≥65s fall annually; exercise reduces fall **rate by 23–34%** |
| Vitamin D | 800–1,000 IU/day where deficient; supplementation reduces falls **only in the deficient** (high-dose bolus dosing increases falls) |

#### Ages 80+ (Confidence: Moderate)

| Item | Numbers |
|---|---|
| Priority order | **Balance and functional/power training ≥ resistance > aerobic volume** — a reversal of the younger-adult ordering |
| Balance | **3×/week, 30 min** (Otago-style: 5 strength + 12 balance exercises + walking 2–3×/week); fall reduction **~35%** |
| Resistance | **2–3×/week, 1–2 sets × 10–15 reps**, machine or body-weight-assisted; **chair-based options** are legitimate |
| Aerobic | Accumulated walking, **even 2–5 min bouts**; no minimum bout length; "as abilities allow" |
| Protein | **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day** if training or in poor health |
| Key evidence | **LIFE study** (n=1,635, mean age 78.9): structured moderate activity (walking to 150 min/wk + strength + balance + flexibility, ~2 center visits + 3–4 home sessions/week) reduced **major mobility disability by 18% (HR 0.82) over 2.6 years**; **persistent mobility disability −28%**. Confidence: **High** for this specific finding |
| Frailty | Multicomponent exercise reverses frailty in a substantial fraction; Vivifrail-style protocols show **large improvements in a 5–8 week timeframe** |
| Supervision | Initial **supervised or professionally prescribed** period is warranted; unsupervised high-challenge balance work carries genuine fall risk during the training itself (Cochrane notes adverse events are generally minor but non-zero) |

**Universal age principle (Confidence: High):** there is **no age at which training stops working.** Nonagenarians in the Fiatarone 1994 trial (mean age 87, n=100) gained **+113% muscle strength**, **+11.8% gait speed**, and **+2.7% thigh muscle area** over 10 weeks of high-intensity resistance training. Relative gains in the very old often *exceed* those of the young because the starting point is so low.

## 12. How People Actually Notice Improvement

This goal category lacks a single headline number (no scale weight, no 1RM PR), so the perception problem is real: **physiological improvement outpaces subjective awareness in the first 2–3 weeks, and subjective awareness of "feeling better" outpaces measurable fitness-test change at 4–8 weeks.** Both mismatches cause dropout at different points.

### 12.1 Timeline of noticeable signals (Confidence: **Moderate**; timelines are Low–Moderate)

| Signal | Detectable | Typical magnitude | How to measure | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|

### 12.2 Recommended submaximal field tests (Confidence: **High** for validity, **Moderate** for MCIDs)

| Test | Protocol | Normative / meaningful change |
|---|---|---|
| **30-second chair stand** | Chair 43–45 cm, arms crossed, count full stands in 30 s | Age 60–64: ~12–17 reps (M), 12–17 (F). **+2 reps = real change**. <8 reps at ≥60 y = elevated fall/frailty risk |
| **6-minute walk test** | Walk max distance in 6 min on a flat 30 m course | Healthy adults **400–700 m**; **MCID ~30 m** (COPD/cardiac) to ~50 m |
| **Rockport 1-mile walk** | Walk 1 mile as fast as possible; record time + ending HR | Estimates VO2max ±10–15%; **−1 min ≈ ~+3–4 mL/kg/min** |
| **3-minute step test (YMCA)** | 12-inch step, 24 steps/min, 3 min, then 1-min seated recovery HR | Recovery HR **−5 to −15 bpm** over 8–12 weeks = clear improvement |
| **Single-leg stance** | Eyes open, hands on hips, time to failure | <10 s at ≥60 y = elevated fall risk; **≥30 s** desirable through the 60s |
| **Timed Up and Go (TUG)** | Rise from chair, walk 3 m, turn, return, sit | **>12 s** = elevated fall risk (≥65 y); **>13.5 s** commonly used cut-point |
| **Grip strength** | Hand dynamometer, best of 3 | **<27 kg (men) / <16 kg (women)** = sarcopenia cut-point (EWGSOP2); strong mortality predictor |
| **Gait speed (4 m)** | Usual pace | **<0.8 m/s** = sarcopenia/frailty flag; **+0.1 m/s = clinically meaningful** |
| **Sit-to-rise test** | Sit to floor and stand, losing 1 point per support used (start 10) | Each 1-point increase associated with **~21% lower all-cause mortality** (Brito 2014, n=2,002) — Confidence: **Moderate**, single cohort |

### 12.3 Practical framing (Confidence: **Low–Moderate**, behavioral synthesis)

- **Weeks 0–3:** almost nothing measurable changes except mood and post-session affect. Expectation-setting here is the single highest-leverage retention intervention. Emphasize **process metrics** (sessions completed, minutes accumulated).
- **Weeks 3–8:** submaximal HR, sleep, energy, and everyday-task ease change. These are the **first credible "it's working" signals** and should be surfaced deliberately, because a scale and a 1RM will both be flat or unimpressive.
- **Weeks 8–16:** field tests, blood pressure, strength, and waist circumference move measurably. Retest cadence of **every 8–12 weeks** matches the signal rate; retesting more often mostly measures noise.
- **Best composite indicator set for this goal:** (1) 7-day average resting HR, (2) one repeatable submaximal test (step test or fixed-route walk with HR), (3) 30-s chair stand, (4) weekly 1–10 energy/sleep self-rating, (5) home BP monthly. This set is cheap, low-noise, and covers cardiac, muscular, functional, and subjective domains.

## 13. Injuries, Limitations, and Chronic Conditions

Training-science principles only; not medical advice. Individuals with unstable cardiac disease, uncontrolled hypertension (≥180/110), recent surgery, or new/undiagnosed symptoms warrant clinical clearance before starting — this is universal across ACSM/AHA guidance.

### 13.1 General principles (Confidence: **Moderate–High**)

1. **Pain-guided loading, not pain avoidance.** For chronic (non-acute) musculoskeletal pain, exercising into **≤5/10 pain that settles to baseline within 24 h and does not worsen week to week** is safe and produces equal or better outcomes than pain-free-only protocols (Smith et al. 2017 *BJSM* meta-analysis: painful exercise showed a **small superiority at short term, SMD 0.27**). Confidence: **Moderate–High**.
2. **Modify the movement, don't delete the pattern.** Range, load, tempo, and implement can nearly always be adjusted to keep the pattern trainable (e.g., box squat, partial ROM, machine, isometric hold).
3. **Isometrics as the entry point** for irritable tendon/joint pain: **5 × 30–45 s at 60–70% MVC, 1–2×/day** — analgesic and load-tolerant.
4. **Load progression, not rest, is the treatment for most tendinopathy.** Heavy slow resistance (**3–4 sets × 6–15 reps, 3 s eccentric / 3 s concentric, 2–3×/week, 12 weeks**) produces outcomes equal to or better than eccentric-only protocols; complete rest is inferior. Confidence: **High**.
5. **Timeframes are long:** tendinopathy **3–6 months**; expect 12 weeks before judging a protocol.
6. **Regress volume, preserve frequency.** When symptoms flare, cut volume **~50%** and hold frequency constant; frequency preserves both adaptation and habit.

### 13.2 Condition-specific numeric anchors

| Condition | Principles / numbers | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Knee osteoarthritis** | Exercise is **first-line** in every major OA guideline (OARSI, ACR, NICE). **2–3×/week, 12 weeks minimum**; effect on pain **SMD ~0.5** and function **~0.5** — comparable to NSAIDs. Both strengthening and aerobic work; **land-based ≈ aquatic**. Higher-dose programs (**≥12 supervised sessions**) outperform lower-dose | High |
| **Chronic low back pain** | Exercise (any type) reduces pain **~10–15 points on a 100-point scale**; **no modality is clearly superior** — adherence dominates. **2–3×/week, ≥8–12 weeks**. Avoid prescribing rest; avoid fear-based movement restriction (Confidence: High that rest is harmful) | High |
| **Shoulder impingement / rotator cuff** | Progressive loading **3×/week, 12 weeks** equals surgical outcomes for non-traumatic cuff-related pain in several RCTs; scapular + cuff strengthening | Moderate–High |
| **Hypertension** | Aerobic **150+ min/wk** + dynamic RT 2–3×/wk; **isometric 4 × 2 min at 30–40% MVC, 3×/wk** as adjunct. Avoid maximal Valsalva-heavy lifting if BP uncontrolled. Expect **−5 to −9 mmHg** | High |
| **Type 2 diabetes** | **150 min/wk aerobic + 2–3×/wk resistance**; **no more than 2 consecutive days without activity** (ADA-specific, because insulin-sensitivity effects last 24–72 h); expect **HbA1c −0.5 to −0.7 pp** for combined training | High |
| **Obesity / high BMI** | Prefer **non-weight-bearing or low-impact** modalities initially (cycling, swimming, elliptical, rowing); joint loading during running is **~2.5–3× body weight** per step. Build to 200–300 min/wk. Resistance training preserves lean mass during weight loss (**~50–90% of the lean loss averted**) | Moderate–High |
| **Long deconditioning (≥6–12 months inactive)** | Start at **50% of the guideline** (e.g., 10–15 min bouts, 2–3×/wk), progress **~10%/week**; expect first 4 weeks to feel disproportionately hard (perceived exertion is elevated before capacity catches up) | Low–Moderate |
| **Osteoporosis / low BMD** | **Avoid** loaded spinal flexion and rapid trunk twisting under load; **include** progressive resistance (≥80% 1RM where tolerated) + impact where safe + balance training. LIFTMOR-type protocols were **safe and effective** in supervised settings | Moderate |
| **Post-cardiac event / cardiac rehab** | Structured cardiac rehab reduces CV mortality **~20–25%**; typical prescription **3×/wk, 20–60 min at 40–80% HRR / RPE 11–14**, ≥12 weeks | High |
| **Pregnancy/postpartum** | **150 min/wk moderate** across ≥3 days (ACOG/WHO); avoid supine-heavy positions after ~16 weeks and contact/fall-risk activities; resistance training is safe and encouraged | High |
| **Hypermobility / joint laxity** | Emphasize **controlled mid-range strengthening and proprioception**; avoid end-range passive stretching. Longer adaptation timeline | Low |

### 13.3 Red flags to stop and seek care (Confidence: **High**, consensus)

Chest pain/pressure; syncope or near-syncope; disproportionate breathlessness at low workloads; new unilateral swelling; night pain unrelated to position; unexplained weight loss; sharp joint pain with locking/giving way; pain that **increases** week over week under a stable load; neurological symptoms (numbness, radiating weakness).

**Landmark individual studies:** Mandsager 2018 *JAMA Netw Open* (CRF and mortality, n=122,007); Stamatakis 2022 *Nature Medicine* (VILPA); Cole 1999 *NEJM* (heart rate recovery); Fiatarone 1994 (nonagenarian resistance training); LIFE Study 2014 *JAMA* (mobility disability in elders); LIFTMOR 2018 (bone loading); PREDIMED 2018 *NEJM* (Mediterranean diet); Windred 2023 (sleep regularity); Brito 2014 (sit-to-rise test); Buist 2008 (graded running progression); Buffey 2022 (postprandial walking).
