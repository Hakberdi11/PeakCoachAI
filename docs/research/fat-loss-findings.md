# Fat Loss with Maximal Muscle and Strength Retention — Evidence Review

**Scope.** A standalone literature review of the most scientifically defensible parameters for reducing body fat while retaining lean mass and strength. Every recommendation is expressed as a number or numeric range. No product, application, or implementation context is assumed or addressed.

**Confidence grading used throughout:**

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **High** | Multiple meta-analyses / systematic reviews with consistent direction, or large well-controlled RCT bodies |
| **Moderate** | Several RCTs with generally consistent findings but meaningful heterogeneity, small samples, or short duration |
| **Low** | Mechanistic reasoning, observational data, single trials, or expert/position-stand consensus without strong trial support |

**A standing caveat on precision.** Most numbers below are derived from group means in trials lasting 4–24 weeks, usually in men, usually in the BMI 22–35 range. Individual variance in TDEE prediction alone is roughly ±10–15% (SD ~200–350 kcal/day around predicted values), so all intake prescriptions should be treated as *starting estimates to be revised against measured rate of change*, not as fixed truths. This point is important enough that it recurs in several sections.

## 1. Energy Balance and Deficit Sizing

### 1.1 The core prescription

| Starting body fat | Recommended deficit (% of TDEE) | Typical absolute deficit | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Men >25% BF / Women >35% BF | 20–30% (up to 35% short-term) | 600–1,000 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Men 15–25% / Women 25–35% | 15–25% | 400–750 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Men 10–15% / Women 20–25% | 10–20% | 300–500 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Men <10% / Women <18–20% | 5–15% | 150–400 kcal/day | Low–Moderate |

**Reasoning and evidence.** Fat mass is the primary buffer against lean tissue catabolism during energy restriction. The Forbes model, and its empirical validations, predicts that the proportion of weight loss coming from fat-free mass (FFM) is an inverse function of initial fat mass: at ~30% body fat roughly 85–95% of weight lost can be fat, whereas at ~8–10% body fat the FFM contribution can rise to 30–50% at the same relative deficit. This is the single strongest justification for scaling deficit magnitude to starting adiposity. *(Source type: modelling studies validated against RCT body-composition data; High for direction, Moderate for exact percentages.)*

**The 500 kcal threshold.** Murphy & Koehler's 2022 meta-analysis and meta-regression (Scand J Med Sci Sports) found energy deficiency significantly impaired lean-mass gains during resistance training (pooled effect ≈ −0.57) but **did not significantly impair strength gains**. The practical inference drawn by the authors: individuals who resistance train and wish to preserve/gain lean mass should generally avoid deficits exceeding **~500 kcal/day**. *(Confidence: Moderate–High. Note this threshold is an absolute number that ignores body size — a 110 kg man at 30% BF and a 55 kg woman at 22% BF are not equivalently stressed by 500 kcal. Expressing the same idea as ~20–25% of TDEE is more defensible.)*

### 1.2 The tradeoff curve

- **Below ~10% of TDEE (≈<250 kcal/day for most adults):** fat loss becomes slow enough (<0.25%BW/week) that it is difficult to distinguish from measurement noise over 2–4 weeks, and adherence suffers from lack of visible feedback. Defensible only for the very lean or for "maingaining"/recomposition intents. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate.)*
- **10–25% of TDEE:** the region where fat loss is meaningful and LBM loss stays minimal in resistance-trained people. Best evidence-supported operating band. *(Moderate.)*
- **25–35% of TDEE:** faster fat loss, still viable at higher starting body fat, but LBM loss risk rises, training performance degrades measurably in most trials, and dropout rates increase. *(Moderate.)*
- **>35–40% of TDEE / VLCD (<800 kcal/day):** meaningfully higher FFM loss fraction (commonly 25–30%+ of weight lost), larger adaptive thermogenesis, and greater weight regain in follow-up. Not defensible for a muscle-retention goal outside medically supervised contexts. *(High for the FFM/regain findings.)*

### 1.3 Aggressive-short vs. mild-long

For an identical total energy deficit accumulated, a **smaller deficit sustained longer** produces equal or better body-composition outcomes than a larger deficit sustained briefly, primarily via reduced LBM loss and better adherence. Counterpoint: adherence *decays with time*, and beyond ~16–20 continuous weeks most cohorts show declining compliance. Practical resolution: **12–16 week deficit blocks at 15–25% of TDEE**, separated by maintenance periods, rather than either extreme. *(Confidence: Moderate; contested at the margins.)*

### 1.4 Setting the starting number

1. Estimate TDEE. Mifflin–St Jeor for RMR (most accurate common predictive equation, ~±10%) × activity factor 1.3–1.9; or better, use 14–16 kcal/lb (31–35 kcal/kg) for active individuals as a maintenance sanity check.
2. Preferred: use **7–14 days of stable-weight intake logging** as the maintenance baseline instead of an equation. Predictive equations misclassify individual TDEE by >200 kcal in ~30–40% of people. *(Moderate.)*
3. Subtract the % from §1.1, then **revise after 2–3 weeks against actual rate of loss** (§2). Roughly, a 1 kg/week deficit ≈ 1,000 kcal/day; 0.5 kg/week ≈ 500 kcal/day — but real-world energy density of tissue lost early in a diet is lower (glycogen + water), so early weeks overstate fat loss.
4. **Floor:** do not prescribe below ~22–25 kcal/kg BW/day for women or ~25–28 kcal/kg/day for men in a training population without clinical oversight; RED-S / Low Energy Availability risk rises sharply below an energy availability of **30 kcal/kg FFM/day** (optimal ≈45 kcal/kg FFM/day). *(Confidence: High for the LEA thresholds — IOC consensus statements.)*

## 2. Rate of Loss

### 2.1 Target rates

| Population | Target rate (%BW/week) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Men >25% BF / Women >35% BF | 0.7–1.0% (up to 1.3% short-term) | Moderate |
| Men 15–25% / Women 25–35% | 0.5–0.75% | Moderate |
| Men 10–15% / Women 20–25% | 0.4–0.6% | Moderate |
| Men <10% / Women <20% (contest prep) | 0.2–0.5% | Low–Moderate |
| Novice trainees (any BF) | 0.4–0.7% (recomp likely; scale weight is a poor signal) | Moderate |

**The 0.7–1.0%/week inflection.** Garthe et al. (2011) compared 0.7%/week vs. 1.4%/week weight loss in elite athletes: the slow group *gained* ~2.1% lean mass and improved 1RM strength; the fast group had no significant LBM gain and inferior performance outcomes. Both lost similar fat mass, but the slow group took roughly twice as long. This is the most-cited direct RCT on rate. Corroborating work (Mero 2010, Huovinen 2015 in lean athletes) is consistent. **Above ~1.0%BW/week, LBM loss rises disproportionately in already-lean, trained individuals; above ~1.4%/week it does so in nearly everyone.** *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

### 2.2 Rate must decline as leanness increases

An empirically useful heuristic: **maximum sustainable fat-loss rate ≈ 0.5–1.0% of *fat mass* per day** in energy terms. At 30% body fat a 90 kg person has 27 kg fat and can plausibly mobilize ~300–500 kcal/kg-fat-based energy readily; at 8% body fat the same person's fat stores can supply far less before net protein catabolism increases. Practically: **halve the target %BW/week roughly each time body fat drops by ~8–10 percentage points.** *(Confidence: Low for the exact formula; Moderate for the direction — this derives from the Alpert/Forbes energy-partitioning literature, not from direct RCTs.)*

### 2.3 Sex adjustment

Because women carry ~8–10 percentage points more essential + storage fat at equivalent "leanness," the *absolute* %BW/week can be similar at matched relative leanness, but women's smaller absolute body mass means the same %BW/week is a smaller kcal deficit and thus harder to create precisely. Women below ~18–20% body fat show menstrual disruption risk at rates >0.7%/week. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

### 2.4 Interpretation window

Never judge rate on <2 weeks of data. Day-to-day body mass varies by **±0.5–2.0 kg (±1–2% BW)** from water, sodium, glycogen (each gram of glycogen binds ~3 g water; a full glycogen swing is ~1.5–2.5 kg of scale weight), GI content, and in menstruating women a luteal-phase fluid shift of **~0.5–2.0 kg**. Use a **7-day rolling average**, and compare rolling averages **14–21 days apart**. *(Confidence: High.)*

## 3. Protein During a Deficit

### 3.1 Targets

| Context | g/kg bodyweight/day | g/kg FFM/day | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| General deficit, higher BF, mild deficit, untrained | 1.6–2.0 | ~2.0–2.4 | High |
| Trained, moderate deficit (15–25% TDEE) | 2.0–2.4 | 2.3–2.8 | High |
| Lean + aggressive deficit (trained, <12% M / <20% F) | 2.4–3.1 | 2.6–3.1 | Moderate |
| Obese (BMI >30) — use FFM or adjusted BW, not total BW | 1.2–1.5 of *total* BW | 1.8–2.2 | Moderate |
| Age ≥60 in a deficit | 1.6–2.2 of BW, min 1.2 | ~2.2–2.6 | High |

**Key references.** Morton et al. 2018 (BMJ, meta-analysis, n=1,863) put the general plateau for resistance-training-related lean gains at **~1.6 g/kg/day** (95% CI upper bound 2.2), but that analysis was overwhelmingly in energy balance. Helms et al. 2014 (systematic review, lean resistance-trained athletes in deficit) recommends **2.3–3.1 g/kg FFM/day**, with the upper end for the leanest and most aggressively dieting. A 2025 updated systematic review with meta-regression (Strength & Conditioning Journal) on energy-restricted resistance-trained individuals confirms a positive dose-response for FFM retention extending above 1.6 g/kg BW. Longland 2016 (2.4 vs 1.2 g/kg, 40% deficit, 4 wk) showed +1.2 kg LBM vs +0.1 kg and greater fat loss with the higher intake. *(Confidence: High that >1.6 g/kg is superior in a deficit; Moderate on the exact top of the range.)*

### 3.2 Why the target rises with leanness and deficit size

Energy restriction suppresses muscle protein synthesis (~15–27% reduction in myofibrillar FSR at ~40% deficits) and, in leaner individuals, increases muscle protein breakdown. Higher leucine/total protein availability partially rescues MPS. Overweight individuals in deficit show largely preserved MPB, which is why a lower g/kg-total-BW target is adequate for them. *(Confidence: Moderate; stable-isotope mechanistic work + small RCTs.)*

### 3.3 Distribution

- **0.3–0.4 g/kg BW per meal**, or **~2.5–3.0 g leucine / 25–40 g high-quality protein per meal**, across **3–5 meals**, spaced 3–5 hours. *(Confidence: Moderate. Total daily protein dominates; distribution is a second-order effect worth perhaps 5–10% of the outcome.)*
- **Pre-sleep 30–40 g casein** modestly increases overnight MPS. Effect on real-world body composition in a deficit is small and inconsistent. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate.)*
- **Peri-workout timing**: the "anabolic window" is now considered a ~4–6 hour band rather than 30–60 minutes; total intake matters more. *(Confidence: High that the narrow window is not supported.)*

### 3.4 Safety ceiling

Intakes up to **3.3–4.4 g/kg/day** have been studied for up to 12 months in trained individuals with healthy kidneys with no adverse renal, hepatic, or lipid outcomes. There is no evidence-based benefit above ~3.1 g/kg for body composition, so the practical ceiling is set by displacement of carbohydrate/fat, not toxicity. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

## 4. Resistance Training During a Deficit

### 4.1 The central finding: preserve intensity/load first

Bryner 1999, and the broader detraining/maintenance literature, show that **mechanical tension at maintained relative load is the primary signal preventing disuse-like atrophy**; reduced load with maintained volume is markedly worse for lean-mass retention than reduced volume with maintained load. Maintenance-training studies (e.g., Bickel 2011) show that in young adults, **1/9th of the original volume (≈1 session/wk, ~1/3 of the sets) at unchanged intensity maintained hypertrophy for 32 weeks**; older adults required ~1/3 of original volume. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

**Rule:** during a cut, **do not reduce working loads to chase reps**. Keep absolute load within **90–100% of pre-diet loads** and allow reps per set to drift down by 1–2 before deloading.

### 4.2 Volume

| Parameter | Prescription | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly hard sets per muscle group — growth (surplus/maintenance) | 10–20 | High |
| Weekly hard sets per muscle group — retention during a deficit | 6–12 (minimum effective floor ≈ **4–6**) | Moderate |
| Frequency per muscle group | 2×/week (2–3× ideal; 1× acceptable at maintenance volumes) | High |
| Sessions per week (total) | 3–5 | High |
| Intensity of load | 70–85% 1RM for the majority of work; ≥1 heavy exposure/wk at 80–90% | Moderate–High |
| Proximity to failure | 0–3 RIR; avoid systematic 0-RIR training in a deficit (recovery is impaired) | Moderate |
| Rep ranges | 5–15 primary; 15–30 acceptable accessory if taken close to failure | High |
| Rest between sets | 2–3 min compound, 1–2 min isolation | Moderate |
| Deload frequency during a deficit | every 6–10 weeks, or reduce volume 40–60% for 1 week keeping load | Low–Moderate |

**Do not add "metabolic"/high-rep circuit work at the expense of heavy loading** as a fat-loss strategy; the additional energy expenditure is small (~5–10 kcal/min) relative to the loss of the retention stimulus. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

### 4.3 Expected strength trajectory

In a well-run 12–16 week cut at ≤0.7%BW/week with adequate protein, expect **maintained or slightly increased 1RM in trained lifters** (Garthe 2011; Murphy 2022 meta showed strength is preserved even when lean-mass gains are impaired). Losses >5% in a main-lift estimated 1RM over 3–4 weeks are a warning signal (see §12). *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

## 5. Cardio Prescription

### 5.1 Dose

| Parameter | Range | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Total weekly aerobic minutes for fat-loss support | 150–300 min moderate-intensity equivalent | High |
| Practical starting point alongside 3–5 lifting sessions | 2–4 sessions, 20–45 min each | Moderate |
| LISS (Zone 2, ~55–70% HRmax, RPE 3–4/10) | 30–60 min/session, 2–5×/wk | Moderate |
| MISS (70–80% HRmax) | 20–40 min/session, 2–3×/wk | Moderate |
| HIIT (≥90% HRmax intervals, e.g., 6–10 × 30–60 s @ 1:1–1:3 work:rest) | 1–3×/wk, 10–25 min total including warm-up | Moderate |
| Ceiling before interference becomes meaningful | **>3 sessions/wk or >20–30 min/session of running-modality endurance** | Moderate |

### 5.2 LISS vs. HIIT

**This is genuinely contested.** Position spread:

- Meta-analyses of HIIT vs. MICT for fat mass (e.g., Keating 2017; Viana 2019) find **no significant difference in total fat loss when energy expenditure is matched**, with HIIT achieving it in ~40–60% less time. Some meta-analyses find a small HIIT advantage for visceral fat.
- HIIT's EPOC advantage is real but small: **~6–15% of the session's energy cost, typically 30–100 kcal**, not the hundreds sometimes claimed. *(Confidence: High.)*
- Practical counterargument favoring LISS during a lifting-focused cut: HIIT (especially running-based) shares recovery resources with resistance training, and its higher RPE reduces adherence in deconditioned populations. LISS is easier to accumulate at high volume and interferes less.
- **Defensible synthesis:** total weekly energy expenditure and adherence dominate modality. Use LISS as the volume base; add ≤2 short HIIT sessions/wk if time-constrained or for VO2max. *(Confidence: High for "modality is secondary"; Moderate for the specific mix.)*

### 5.3 Interference effect

Wilson et al. 2012 (meta-analysis, 21 studies, 422 effect sizes) found interference scales with **frequency, duration, and modality**: running produced significant decrements in strength and hypertrophy; **cycling did not**. Effects are largely limb-specific (lower-body endurance impairs lower-body resistance adaptation). Interference for hypertrophy became detectable around **>3 endurance sessions/wk and >30 min/session**; power/rate-of-force-development is the outcome most affected, hypertrophy less so, maximal strength least. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

**Mitigation parameters:**
- **Separation:** ≥6 h between a lifting session and a hard endurance session; **≥24 h** is preferable for high-intensity lower-body endurance. Interference is minimal at ≥24 h separation. *(Moderate.)*
- **Order:** if performed in the same session, do **resistance first** when strength/hypertrophy is the priority. Intra-session-order meta-analyses show the second-performed modality is attenuated. *(Moderate–High.)*
- **Modality choice:** prefer cycling, elliptical, incline walking, rowing (low eccentric load) over downhill/high-eccentric running for lifters. *(Moderate.)*
- **Ceiling:** treat **~150–200 min/wk of moderate cardio** as the point beyond which additional deficit should preferentially come from diet or NEAT rather than more structured cardio, for lifters. *(Low–Moderate; expert consensus.)*

### 5.4 Cardio vs. diet for creating the deficit

Combined diet + exercise beats either alone for fat loss with LBM retention. Meta-analytic estimates: diet alone loses ~20–30% of weight as LBM; diet + resistance training reduces that to **~5–10%** (in obese older adults, resistance training abolished ~93.5% of the caloric-restriction-induced LBM loss). *(Confidence: High.)*

## 6. NEAT and Daily Activity

### 6.1 Magnitude

- NEAT accounts for **~15% of TDEE in sedentary individuals and up to 50% in highly active ones**; between-individual variation in NEAT spans **~2,000 kcal/day**. *(Confidence: High — Levine's whole-room-calorimeter and doubly-labelled-water work.)*
- TDEE components (typical): BMR 60–70%, TEF 8–12%, EAT (structured exercise) 0–10%, NEAT 15–30%.
- In overfeeding studies, NEAT changes explained ~10-fold differences in fat gain. In *under*feeding, NEAT falls: measured reductions of **100–500 kcal/day**, with spontaneous physical activity dropping meaningfully within 1–3 weeks of restriction. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*
- Step counts commonly fall **15–30% (≈1,500–3,000 steps/day)** during unmonitored dieting — often without the dieter noticing. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

### 6.2 Step targets

| Goal | Steps/day | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum to defend against diet-induced NEAT suppression | **7,000–8,000** | Moderate |
| Standard fat-loss target | **8,000–12,000** | Moderate |
| Aggressive/lean-phase or high-BF individuals with low structured cardio | 12,000–15,000 | Low–Moderate |
| Energy value | ~30–50 kcal per 1,000 steps (0.04–0.06 kcal/kg/step); 10,000 steps ≈ 300–500 kcal | Moderate |

Mortality/health benefit curves plateau around **7,000–9,000 steps/day** (Paluch 2022 meta-analysis), which conveniently anchors the lower bound. *(Confidence: High for the health-outcome plateau; Moderate for its use as a fat-loss floor.)*

### 6.3 Countermeasures

1. **Set an explicit step floor and track it** — activity that is not measured reliably drifts down. Holding steps constant is the single most controllable defense against adaptive NEAT suppression. *(Moderate.)*
2. **Re-audit step average every 2 weeks**; a decline >10% from baseline explains a plateau more often than "metabolic damage."
3. Cheap accumulation: 10 min walking ≈ 1,000–1,200 steps ≈ 40–60 kcal.
4. Note that wearable step counts have **±5–15% device error** and are not comparable across devices — trend within one device only. *(High.)*

## 7. Diet Breaks and Refeeds

Definitions used here: **refeed** = 1–2 day increase to (near) maintenance, usually carbohydrate-driven; **diet break** = 7–14+ consecutive days at maintenance.

### 7.1 Evidence

- **MATADOR (Byrne 2018, n=51 obese men, 30% deficit):** 16 weeks continuous restriction vs. 2-week-on/2-week-off intermittent restriction matched for total restriction weeks. Intermittent group lost **12.3 kg vs 8.0 kg fat**, retained more FFM, and showed less adaptive thermogenesis; at 6-month follow-up the intermittent group had maintained **~10.5 kg vs ~4.5 kg** more loss. *(Confidence: Moderate — a single well-run RCT with a large effect, not yet replicated at that magnitude.)*
- **ICECAP / other intermittent-restriction trials** in overweight populations often show **no advantage** for intermittent vs. continuous when total deficit is matched. Several systematic reviews (e.g., Seimon 2015; Headland 2016) conclude intermittent energy restriction is **equivalent, not superior**, to continuous restriction for weight/fat loss.
- **Refeeds:** Campbell et al. 2020 (n=27 trained, 7 weeks, 2 consecutive high-carb refeed days/week vs. continuous deficit, matched weekly energy) found **greater FFM retention in the refeed group** (−0.1 kg vs −1.0 kg) with similar fat loss. Small, single study. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate.)*
- **Leptin rebound:** a single high-carbohydrate day raises leptin ~30% acutely, but levels fall back within ~24–48 h of resuming the deficit. The endocrine case for weekly refeeds is therefore weak; the psychological/adherence and glycogen/training-performance case is stronger. *(Confidence: Moderate for the leptin kinetics; Low for downstream fat-loss benefit.)*

### 7.2 Practical parameters

| Parameter | Prescription | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Max continuous deficit before a planned break | **8–16 weeks** (shorter — 6–8 wk — the leaner the dieter or larger the deficit) | Low–Moderate |
| Diet break length | **7–14 days at maintenance** (estimated maintenance, not "free eating") | Moderate |
| Diet break calorie level | TDEE ± 0–5%; typically +20–30% above deficit intake, mostly from carbohydrate | Moderate |
| Refeed frequency | 1–2 days/week, or 1 day per 7–14 days | Low–Moderate |
| Refeed size | +20–40% kcal over deficit intake, carbohydrate raised to **4–8 g/kg BW/day**, fat held low, protein unchanged | Low–Moderate |
| Expected scale rebound on a break | +1–2 kg (glycogen + water + gut content), resolving in 3–7 days after resuming | High |
| Deficit-phase-to-maintenance ratio over a long cut | roughly **3:1 to 6:1** weeks | Low |

**Where experts disagree:** Some (Byrne, Trexler, Helms) treat scheduled breaks as important for attenuating adaptive thermogenesis and preserving FFM. Others (Hall, and several intermittent-restriction reviewers) argue the evidence for a metabolic advantage is thin and that breaks work mainly by improving adherence and reducing total deficit exposure. **Both camps agree breaks are not harmful and are useful for adherence**; the disagreement is over whether there is a metabolic mechanism beyond that. Report the honest range rather than picking a side. *(Confidence in the disagreement itself: High.)*

## 8. Sleep

### 8.1 Body-composition effect

**Nedeltcheva 2010** (Ann Intern Med; n=10, crossover, 14 days each, identical ~90% maintenance restriction, 5.5 h vs 8.5 h TIB): identical total weight loss, but

- Fat loss: **1.4 kg (8.5 h) vs 0.6 kg (5.5 h)** — a **55% reduction in the proportion of weight lost as fat**
- FFM loss: **1.5 kg vs 2.4 kg** — a **60% increase in lean-mass loss**
- Reduced fat oxidation and higher hunger/ghrelin in the restricted condition

*(Confidence: Moderate — dramatic effect but n=10, short, and highly controlled. Directionally supported by Wang 2018 (n=36, 8 wk) and Chaput's reviews.)*

### 8.2 Appetite hormones and intake

- Short sleep (~4–5 h vs 8–10 h): **ghrelin +15–28%, leptin −15–18%**, increased hunger and appetite ratings, with the largest increases for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods. (Spiegel 2004; Taheri 2004.) *(Confidence: Moderate–High for the hormone shifts; Moderate for their causal link to intake.)*
- Free-living sleep restriction increases **ad libitum energy intake by ~250–385 kcal/day** (meta-analysis, Al Khatib 2017), without a compensatory increase in expenditure. *(Confidence: High.)*
- Sleep-extension RCTs (Tasali 2022): extending habitual short sleepers by ~1.2 h/night reduced intake by **~270 kcal/day** over 2 weeks. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

### 8.3 Prescription

| Parameter | Target | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Total sleep time | **7–9 h/night** | High |
| Minimum before body-composition penalty appears | **<6 h/night** is the clear risk zone; 6–7 h is intermediate | Moderate |
| Consistency of sleep/wake times | ±30–60 min | Moderate |
| Nap compensation | 20–30 min naps recover some performance but do not fully offset chronic restriction | Low |
| Effect size worth quoting | short sleep can shift the fat:lean loss ratio from ~50:50 toward ~20:80 at an identical deficit | Moderate |

**Practical implication:** for a person sleeping <6 h, fixing sleep is plausibly a larger lever on *composition* of loss than a further 200 kcal reduction in intake. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate as a comparative claim; the underlying components are better supported than the comparison.)*

## 9. Nutrition Beyond Protein

### 9.1 Carbohydrate and fat floors

| Nutrient | Floor during a deficit | Rationale | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fat | **0.5–1.0 g/kg BW/day**, and not below **15–20% of total kcal** | Essential fatty acids, fat-soluble vitamin absorption, sex-hormone substrate | Moderate |
| Carbohydrate (resistance-training performance floor) | **3–5 g/kg BW/day** typical; hard floor **~2 g/kg** for lifters, **~100 g/day** absolute | Glycogen for training volume; below this, set-volume and RPE degrade | Moderate |
| Carbohydrate for endurance-heavy concurrent training | 5–8 g/kg/day | Higher glycogen turnover | High |
| Fiber | **14 g per 1,000 kcal**, or **25–38 g/day**; practical 25–40 g | Satiety, GI, glycemic control | Moderate–High |

**Method:** set protein first (§3), then fat at its floor-to-preference level, then allocate all remaining calories to carbohydrate. When calories are very low, protein + minimum fat can consume most of the budget — this is a signal the deficit is too aggressive.

**Low-carb vs. low-fat:** meta-analyses (Hall & Guo 2017; Johnston 2014; DIETFITS 2018) show **no meaningful difference in fat loss when protein and calories are matched** (differences ≈0.5 kg, within measurement error, favoring higher-fat marginally in some isocaloric metabolic-ward work and favoring low-fat marginally in others). Choose by adherence. *(Confidence: High.)*

### 9.2 Meal frequency and timing

- **Meal frequency (3 vs 6 meals, calories/protein matched):** no significant effect on fat loss or FFM retention in meta-analysis once protein is controlled. Range of practice: **3–6 eating occasions/day**. *(Confidence: High that frequency per se is not important; Moderate that ≥3 protein feedings ≥0.3 g/kg matters.)*
- **Intermittent fasting / TRE:** meta-analyses show TRE produces weight loss comparable to continuous restriction when calories are matched; standalone benefit is largely via spontaneous calorie reduction (~200–550 kcal/day). Some concern that longer fasts with lower protein feeding frequency slightly disadvantage FFM retention in lifters. Feeding windows of **8–10 h** are the most studied. *(Confidence: Moderate. Contested: whether TRE has any calorie-independent metabolic benefit — recent well-controlled trials mostly say no.)*
- **Breakfast skipping / nutrient timing:** no established independent effect on fat loss at matched intake. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*
- **Pre/post-training nutrition:** for lifters in a deficit, having **0.3–0.4 g/kg protein plus 0.5–1.0 g/kg carbohydrate within ~2 h either side of training** is a reasonable, low-cost performance practice — not a large body-composition lever. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate.)*

### 9.3 Alcohol

- **7.1 kcal/g** (~5.6–6.9 kcal/g net after thermic effect of ~10–30%, which is higher and more variable than for other macronutrients).
- Practical energy: standard drink ≈ **100–150 kcal**; beer 150–200 kcal/355 ml; wine ~120–125 kcal/150 ml.
- **Suppresses fat oxidation** for several hours post-ingestion (whole-body lipid oxidation reduced ~30–40% after ~24 g alcohol) and is prioritized for oxidation, effectively promoting fat storage of concurrently eaten dietary fat. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*
- **Acute testosterone suppression:** trivial at ≤0.5 g/kg; meaningful (−20 to −25% over subsequent hours) at **≥1.0–1.5 g/kg** (roughly ≥5–7 drinks). *(Confidence: Moderate.)*
- **MPS:** ~1.5 g/kg alcohol post-exercise reduced myofibrillar protein synthesis by **~24–37%** even when protein was co-ingested (Parr 2014). *(Confidence: Moderate, single strong study.)*
- **Disinhibition:** alcohol increases same-occasion energy intake by ~11–30%.
- **Practical ceiling for a muscle-retention cut:** **≤2 standard drinks/occasion, ≤3–4/week, and not within ~8 h before or after a hard training session**; ideally 0 during aggressive phases. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate; a consensus synthesis.)*

### 9.4 Hydration

- **~30–40 ml/kg BW/day** total water (food + fluid), i.e. roughly 2.0–3.5 L/day for most adults; add **0.5–1.0 L per hour of training** and 1.25–1.5 L per kg of exercise-induced body-mass loss for rehydration. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*
- **Pre-meal water (500 ml, 30 min before meals)** reduced intake and increased weight loss by ~1.3–2 kg over 12 weeks in RCTs. Small but real. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*
- **≥2% body-mass dehydration** impairs endurance performance measurably; strength/power effects appear closer to **3–4%**. *(Confidence: High for endurance; Moderate for strength.)*
- Sodium: sudden sodium changes shift body mass by **0.5–2 kg** within 48 h with no fat change — a major source of scale noise, not a fat-loss lever.

### 9.5 Micronutrients worth flagging in a deficit

Iron (menstruating women), vitamin D (2,000–4,000 IU/day if deficient), calcium (1,000–1,200 mg/day), and total micronutrient density — restriction below ~1,500 kcal/day makes RDA coverage difficult without deliberate planning or a multivitamin. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

## 10. Plateaus and Metabolic Adaptation

### 10.1 Expected magnitude

| Component | Typical magnitude | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Total TDEE decline after 10% weight loss | **~20–25%**, of which ~10–15% is explained by lost tissue mass | High |
| Adaptive thermogenesis (decline *beyond* that predicted by body-composition change) | **~50–150 kcal/day** typically; **10–15% below predicted** in more extreme cases | Moderate–High |
| Extreme case (Biggest Loser, 30 wk, huge deficit) | ~500 kcal/day below predicted RMR, persisting 6 years | Moderate (n=14, extreme, poorly generalizable) |
| Athlete/physique-prep case | RMR 5–10% below predicted after contest prep; largely recovers within 1–4 months of refeeding | Moderate |
| Contribution from reduced NEAT/SPA | often the **largest single component**, 100–500 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Reduced TEF (from eating less) | ~10% of the calorie reduction, e.g. −50 kcal on a 500 kcal cut | High |

**Reframe:** "metabolic damage" is not supported; measurable, largely reversible adaptation of 50–150 kcal/day plus a much larger, behaviorally-driven reduction in activity and an underestimation of intake is the honest picture. Doubly-labelled-water studies consistently show self-reported intake underestimated by **20–40%** in plateaued dieters. *(Confidence: High.)*

### 10.2 Distinguishing a true plateau from noise

A true plateau requires **all** of:
1. **≥3 weeks** (preferably 3–4) of 7-day rolling-average bodyweight showing <0.2–0.3% BW/week change (i.e., trend slope indistinguishable from zero);
2. Waist circumference unchanged within measurement error (**±1.0–1.5 cm**, see §12) over the same window;
3. Verified adherence — logged intake and step count matching prescription within ~10%.

Anything shorter than 2–3 weeks, or where waist is still falling while scale weight is flat, is noise or a recomposition/water-retention artifact (notably the "whoosh"/Supercompensation pattern, and cortisol-driven fluid retention that can mask 1–2 kg of fat loss for 1–3 weeks). *(Confidence: Moderate; measurement-statistics reasoning plus practitioner consensus.)*

### 10.3 Ordered response hierarchy

1. **Audit intake accuracy first** (re-weigh food for 7 days). Expected yield: 200–500 kcal/day of unlogged intake in a substantial fraction of cases. *(High.)*
2. **Restore activity**: bring steps back to the prescribed floor; expected yield 100–300 kcal/day. *(Moderate.)*
3. **Recalculate TDEE from observed data**: new deficit = current intake − (observed maintenance). Reduce intake by **5–10% (≈100–250 kcal/day)**, not more, and only if intake remains above the floors in §1.4 and §9.1. *(Moderate.)*
4. **Add cardio**: +2 sessions of 20–30 min, ≈ +300–600 kcal/week, staying under the interference ceiling (§5.3). *(Moderate.)*
5. **If ≥8–12 weeks in deficit, or intake is at the floor, take a 7–14 day diet break** rather than cutting further. *(Moderate.)*

Never respond to a single flat week with a calorie cut.

## 11. Individual Modifiers

### 11.1 Sex

**Well-supported differences:**
- Essential body fat: **~3–5% (men) vs ~8–12% (women)**; healthy athletic ranges ~6–13% vs ~14–20%. Absolute body-fat targets must be offset by **~8–10 percentage points**. *(High.)*
- Fat distribution: women store proportionally more gluteofemoral (subcutaneous) fat, men more visceral/abdominal; men lose visceral fat faster early in a diet, which produces faster waist-circumference change for the same %BW lost. *(High.)*
- Lower absolute TDEE (smaller body mass and FFM) means **the same %-of-TDEE deficit is a smaller absolute kcal number** (e.g., 20% of 2,000 = 400 vs 20% of 3,000 = 600), leaving less room before hitting intake floors. *(High.)*
- Menstrual-cycle fluid shifts of **0.5–2.0 kg** in the luteal phase; energy expenditure rises ~2–8% (≈50–150 kcal/day) luteally, and appetite/cravings increase. Best practice: **compare rolling averages at the same cycle phase (4-week comparisons), not week-to-week.** *(Moderate–High.)*
- Low energy availability risk (**<30 kcal/kg FFM/day**) produces menstrual dysfunction and bone-density loss more readily in women; the female athlete triad / RED-S literature is the strongest sex-specific safety constraint. *(High.)*

**Weakly supported claims that are commonly asserted:**
- "Women need lower protein per kg." Not supported; if anything women may oxidize slightly less protein in exercise, but prescriptions do not differ meaningfully. *(Confidence: High that no adjustment is warranted.)*
- "Women should train higher reps / lighter." Not supported; sex differences in hypertrophic and strength response to standard programming are small to nil in meta-analysis (relative strength gains are similar or slightly greater in women in upper-body training). Women may recover slightly faster between sets and tolerate slightly higher volume/frequency, but the effect is small. *(Moderate.)*
- "Women lose fat more slowly." Absolute rates are lower because bodies are smaller; **relative** (%BW/week) rates are similar at matched relative leanness. *(Moderate.)*
- "Fasted cardio is worse/better for women." Insufficient evidence either way. *(Low.)*
- Sex-specific programming around menstrual phase (e.g., loading follicular, deloading luteal) has **weak and inconsistent trial support**; a 2020+ series of reviews rates the evidence as low-quality. Present as optional, not prescriptive. *(Confidence: High that the evidence is weak.)*

### 11.2 Training experience

| Group | Simultaneous fat loss + muscle gain? | Realistic LBM change over a 12-wk deficit | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained/novice (<6–12 mo) | Yes, reliably — "newbie recomp" | **+1.0 to +3.0 kg** LBM while losing fat, at deficits up to 20–25% TDEE | Moderate–High |
| Intermediate (1–3 yr) | Sometimes, at deficits ≤15–20% | −0.5 to +1.0 kg | Moderate |
| Advanced (3+ yr, near genetic ceiling) | Rarely; goal is retention | −1.0 to 0 kg (target: ≥ −0.5 kg) | Moderate |
| Returning after detraining ("muscle memory") | Yes, strongly — myonuclear retention | +1.0 to +3.0 kg, rapid | Moderate |
| Overweight + untrained | Best case of all | +1.5 to +3.0 kg reported in several RCTs | Moderate–High |

Annualized natural hypertrophy potential (Lyle McDonald / Alan Aragon models, expert consensus not RCT): year 1 ≈ 9–11 kg (men) / 4.5–5.5 kg (women); year 2 ≈ half that; year 3 ≈ half again; year 4+ ≈ 1–2 kg/yr. In a deficit, expect **~0–50% of the equivalent surplus rate**. *(Confidence: Low — model-based expert consensus.)*

### 11.3 Age

| Parameter | Adjustment | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| RMR decline | **~1–2% per decade after age 20**, largely explained by FFM loss; Pontzer 2021 (Science, n=6,421, DLW) found **size-adjusted TDEE is stable from ~20 to ~60**, then declines **~0.7%/year** | High |
| Sarcopenia baseline | **~3–8% muscle mass loss per decade after 30**, accelerating to ~1–2%/yr after 60–70 | High |
| Protein target ≥50–60 yr | **1.6–2.2 g/kg/day** (vs ~1.6 in young adults); per-meal leucine threshold rises from ~2.5 g to **3.0–4.0 g**, i.e. **0.4–0.6 g/kg protein per meal** | High |
| Anabolic resistance | MPS response to a given protein dose is blunted; needs larger per-meal doses and more resistance training stimulus | High |
| Deficit size ≥60 yr | Cap at **10–20% of TDEE**; 200–500 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Rate of loss ≥60 yr | **0.25–0.5%BW/week** (higher rates markedly increase FFM and bone loss) | Moderate |
| Resistance training ≥60 yr | Non-negotiable; **2–3×/wk, ≥2 sets/muscle, 60–80% 1RM**; volume maintenance requires ~1/3 of accumulation volume vs. ~1/9 in young adults | High |
| Recovery | Add ~24 h between hard sessions for the same muscle vs. young adults; deload every 4–8 wk | Low–Moderate |
| Adolescents (<18) | Deficits should be minimal (0–10%) and growth-preserving; not addressed further here | Low |

### 11.4 Starting body composition

| Starting body fat (M / F) | Deficit | Rate | Protein (g/kg BW) | Recomp likely? | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| >30% / >40% | 25–35% TDEE | 0.8–1.2%BW/wk | 1.2–1.6 (of total BW) or 2.0–2.4 g/kg FFM | Yes | Moderate |
| 25–30% / 35–40% | 20–30% | 0.7–1.0% | 1.6–2.0 | Often | Moderate |
| 18–25% / 28–35% | 15–25% | 0.5–0.8% | 1.8–2.2 | Sometimes | Moderate |
| 12–18% / 22–28% | 12–20% | 0.4–0.7% | 2.0–2.4 | Rarely | Moderate |
| <12% / <22% | 5–15% | 0.2–0.5% | 2.4–3.1 | No | Low–Moderate |

Underlying principle (Forbes): the fraction of weight loss coming from FFM is inversely proportional to initial fat mass. This single relationship justifies almost every row above. Practical corollary: **a lean person's diet must be slower, higher-protein, and shorter in duration than a higher-body-fat person's.** *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

**Lower bounds worth stating:** sustained body fat below ~5–6% (men) / ~12–14% (women) is associated with hormonal suppression, mood/sleep disturbance, and performance decline; these are competition-day states, not maintainable ones. *(Confidence: Moderate–High; contest-prep case-series literature — Rossow 2013, Kistler 2014, Pardue 2017.)*

## 12. How Progress Is Actually Perceived and Measured

### 12.1 The problem being solved

A 500 kcal/day deficit ≈ 0.45 kg/week ≈ 65 g/day of true tissue change — an order of magnitude smaller than the **±0.5–2.0 kg** of daily noise from water, glycogen, sodium, gut content, and (in women) cycle phase. **Day-to-day scale weight has a signal-to-noise ratio well below 1.** Any progress framework must therefore rely on multi-day aggregation plus non-scale signals.

### 12.2 Ranked reliability of signals

| Signal | Detection latency (typical, at 0.5–0.75%BW/wk) | Noise / error | Reliability rank | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **7-day rolling-average bodyweight** | 10–14 days | ±0.2–0.3 kg on the average | **1 (most reliable overall)** | High |
| **Waist circumference (navel or narrowest)** | 2–4 weeks | TEM ~0.5–1.4 cm intra-observer (self-measured slightly worse); use **±1.0–1.5 cm** as the noise band | **2** | Moderate–High |
| **Strength/performance trend** (as a *lean-retention* signal, not a fat-loss signal) | 3–4 weeks | ±2–5% session-to-session | **3 (best lean-mass proxy)** | Moderate |
| **Clothing fit (waistband)** | 3–6 weeks | qualitative, threshold-based | 4 | Low–Moderate |
| **Progress photos** | 4–8 weeks | large lighting/posture/pump artifacts if uncontrolled | 5 (best for *long*-interval comparison) | Moderate |
| **Bioimpedance body fat %** | unreliable at these timescales | ±3–5% BF absolute; hydration-sensitive | Low value week-to-week | High (that it is noisy) |
| **DXA** | 8–12 weeks | ±1–2% BF; ±0.5–1.0 kg FM precision | good but infrequent/expensive | High |
| **Skinfolds (trained tester, 7-site)** | 3–4 weeks | TEM ~1–2 mm/site, ~1–1.5% BF | good if the same tester | Moderate |

### 12.3 Circumference protocol (to get error down to the usable range)

- **Sites:** waist (both at umbilicus and at the narrowest point), hips (widest gluteal), and for muscle-retention tracking, upper arm (mid-biceps, relaxed) and mid-thigh.
- **Conditions to standardize:** same time of day (**morning, fasted, post-void**), same body position (standing, relaxed, arms at sides), **at the end of a normal exhale** (not sucked in, not pushed out), tape horizontal and snug without indenting skin, same tape (a spring-tension tape reduces error).
- **Replicates:** take **2–3 measurements per site and average**; if two readings differ by >0.5 cm, take a third.
- **Cadence:** **weekly or biweekly**, never daily.
- **Minimum meaningful change:** **≥1.0–1.5 cm** at the waist before treating it as real; over 4 weeks at 0.5–0.75%BW/week, expect **1–3 cm** of waist reduction. Waist tends to fall faster than hips early in a diet, especially in men (visceral fat mobilizes first).
- Useful ratio anchors: waist-to-height ratio target **<0.5**; a 1 cm waist reduction corresponds very roughly to **~1 kg of fat loss** in the 0.7–1.3 kg range, sex- and site-dependent. *(Confidence: Moderate.)*

### 12.4 Progress photos

- **Cadence: every 2–4 weeks.** Weekly photos change less than the photographic noise floor and tend to demoralize.
- **Validity conditions:** same time of day (morning, fasted, post-void), same room and **same artificial lighting** (natural light varies hugely), same distance and camera height (~chest height, phone braced or on a timer), same clothing (minimal, same garment), same 4 poses (front relaxed, back relaxed, both sides relaxed) held identically, **relaxed, not flexed** (add flexed shots separately for muscle tracking), and no pre-photo pump/training within ~2 h.
- **Detection:** visible change generally appears after **~2–3 kg (4–7 lb) of fat loss**, i.e. **4–6 weeks** at typical rates; changes are noticed by *others* after roughly **~4 kg / 8–9 weeks**. Facial change tends to be noticed first by observers. *(Confidence: Low–Moderate; small perceptual studies plus practitioner consensus.)*

### 12.5 Clothing fit

- One clothing size ≈ **2.5–5 cm (1–2 inches)** of waist circumference ≈ **~3–5 kg** of fat loss. Expect a size change roughly every **6–12 weeks** at 0.5–0.75%BW/week.
- Belt-notch tracking (one notch ≈ 2.5 cm) is a cheap, low-error proxy with a **6–10 week** latency.
- *(Confidence: Low–Moderate; anthropometric-to-garment mapping, not RCT data.)*

### 12.6 Strength/performance as the lean-retention warning system

This is the highest-value *qualitative-to-quantitative* signal for the specific goal of muscle retention:

| Pattern over 3–4 weeks | Interpretation | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Bodyweight falling 0.5–0.75%/wk, estimated 1RM / rep-PRs flat or rising | Ideal: fat loss with lean retention | No change |
| Weight falling, e1RM down **<3–5%** | Acceptable; largely fatigue/glycogen | Monitor; consider a refeed |
| Weight falling, e1RM down **>5–10%**, or reps at a fixed load down ≥2 for 2+ consecutive sessions across multiple lifts | Warning: likely excessive deficit, under-recovery, or protein/sleep deficit | Reduce deficit by 10–20%, verify protein ≥2.0 g/kg, verify sleep ≥7 h, deload |
| Weight flat, strength falling | Under-recovery without fat loss — worst quadrant | Diet break |
| Weight flat, waist falling, strength rising | Recomposition — success despite a flat scale | No change; stop weighing as the primary metric |

Track **estimated 1RM (Epley/Brzycki) on 2–4 index lifts** and total weekly hard-set volume; judge on a **3–4 week moving comparison**, not session to session. Bar-speed/RPE drift at a fixed load is an earlier but noisier indicator. *(Confidence: Moderate; supported by Garthe 2011, Murphy 2022, and contest-prep case series showing strength preservation at slow rates and strength loss at fast ones.)*

### 12.7 Realistic timelines summary

| Milestone | Typical time at 0.5–0.75%BW/week |
|---|---|
| Scale trend becomes readable above noise | 10–14 days |
| First reliable waist change (≥1.5 cm) | 2–4 weeks |
| Self-noticeable change in photos/mirror | 4–6 weeks |
| Clothing fit change (one belt notch) | 6–10 weeks |
| Noticed by other people | 8–12 weeks |
| One clothing size | 6–12 weeks |
| 5% bodyweight (clinically meaningful) | 7–10 weeks |
| 10% bodyweight | 14–20 weeks (usually requires a diet break inside it) |

*(Confidence: Moderate; arithmetic from rate targets plus perceptual-threshold estimates.)*

## 13. Injuries and Physical Limitations — General Training-Science Principles

*(Not medical advice. These are programming principles, not clinical prescriptions; any acute, undiagnosed, or worsening pain warrants professional assessment.)*

### 13.1 General framework

| Principle | Numeric parameter | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel) | Train within **≤3–4/10 pain** during exercise; pain must settle to baseline within **24 h** and not be worse the next morning | Moderate |
| Load reduction when modifying | Start at **40–60% of prior load**, progress **5–10%/week** if the pain rule above is satisfied | Low–Moderate |
| Preserve the *unaffected* limbs/regions | Maintain full volume/intensity elsewhere; **cross-education** transfers **~8–12% strength** to an immobilized contralateral limb | Moderate–High |
| Minimum stimulus to prevent atrophy in a limited region | **1–2 sessions/wk, 2–4 sets, ≥30% 1RM if taken near failure**, or **20–40% 1RM with BFR** | Moderate |
| Blood-flow-restriction training when heavy load is contraindicated | **20–40% 1RM, 4 sets (30-15-15-15), 40–80% limb occlusion pressure, 2–3×/wk** — produces hypertrophy comparable to 70% 1RM training | Moderate–High |
| Isometrics for tendon-related pain | **5 × 45 s at ~70% MVIC, 2–3×/day** for analgesia; heavy slow resistance (**3–4 sets, 6–15 reps, 3 s up / 3 s down, 3×/wk**) for remodeling | Moderate |
| Range-of-motion modification | Train the **pain-free ROM at full intensity** rather than full ROM at reduced intensity; partial ROM at long muscle lengths retains most hypertrophic stimulus | Moderate |
| Return-to-load progression | ~**10% weekly increase** in load or volume as an upper bound (the "10% rule" is a heuristic, weakly evidenced but conservative) | Low |

### 13.2 Cardio substitution ladder (descending joint load)

1. Swimming / water walking — near-zero impact, ~0.2× bodyweight loading
2. Cycling / recumbent bike — no impact, minimal eccentric load, lowest interference with lifting
3. Elliptical / arm ergometer (upper-body-only option preserves lower-limb rest)
4. Rowing — low impact but high lumbar demand; unsuitable for many low-back cases
5. Incline walking (**10–15% grade, 4.5–5.5 km/h**) — raises energy cost ~50–100% vs. flat walking at the same speed with lower speed-related impact
6. Flat walking
7. Running — highest impact (**2.5–3.0× bodyweight ground reaction force** vs 1.0–1.5× walking) and the modality with the strongest interference signal

Ground-reaction-force reduction of ~30–50% is available simply by substituting cycling or swimming for running at matched energy expenditure. *(Confidence: Moderate–High.)*

### 13.3 Specific-region heuristics

- **Low back:** avoid loaded spinal flexion under fatigue; substitute trap-bar/hex deadlift, belt squat, chest-supported rows, split squats; retain 2–3 heavy sessions/wk of unaffected patterns. McGill "Big 3" as tolerance work, not as the primary stimulus.
- **Knee:** reduce knee-flexion ROM to the pain-free range; step-ups, leg press with restricted depth, Spanish squats, Nordic/leg-curl work for posterior chain; cycling with **higher saddle height** reduces patellofemoral load.
- **Shoulder:** replace overhead pressing with landmine/incline press (30–45°); neutral-grip variants; scapular-control and rotator-cuff work at **2–3×/wk, 2–3 sets, 15–25 reps**.
- **Obesity/high BMI (>35):** prioritize **non-weight-bearing cardio** and machine-based resistance training; step targets may need to start at **4,000–6,000/day** and progress **~500–1,000 steps/week**; joint loading rather than cardiovascular capacity is usually the limiter.
- **Pregnancy/postpartum, cardiac, metabolic, and eating-disorder histories** are outside the scope of a general training prescription and require individualized clinical input; deficits are often contraindicated entirely.

### 13.4 Deficit interaction

Energy restriction impairs tissue healing (collagen synthesis, bone remodeling) and increases stress-fracture risk, particularly under low energy availability (**<30 kcal/kg FFM/day**). During active injury rehabilitation: cap the deficit at **0–15% of TDEE**, keep protein at the **upper** end (**2.0–2.5 g/kg**), and consider **1.5–3.0 g/day HMB** or **10–20 g collagen + 50 mg vitamin C, 30–60 min pre-loading** for tendon/ligament contexts (evidence Low–Moderate). *(Confidence: Moderate for the LEA/healing link; Low for the supplements.)*

## 15. Where the Evidence Is Genuinely Contested

State these as ranges, never as settled facts:

1. **Diet breaks / intermittent restriction** — MATADOR shows a large advantage; several systematic reviews show equivalence. Consensus floor: not harmful, probably helpful for adherence; a distinct metabolic advantage is unproven.
2. **Refeed necessity** — one supportive RCT for FFM retention; leptin kinetics argue against a strong endocrine mechanism. Range of expert opinion: "essential every week" (physique-coach camp) to "unnecessary, just eat consistently" (energy-balance researcher camp).
3. **HIIT vs. LISS** — matched-energy meta-analyses show no difference in fat loss; HIIT wins on time efficiency and VO2max, LISS wins on recovery cost and adherence in deconditioned people.
4. **Meal timing / frequency / TRE** — no calorie-independent effect is well established; effects seen in free-living trials are best explained by spontaneous intake reduction.
5. **Optimal protein ceiling** — 1.6 g/kg (meta-analytic plateau in energy balance) vs. up to 3.1 g/kg (deficit-specific reviews in lean athletes). The deficit-specific higher numbers rest on fewer, smaller trials.
6. **Magnitude and persistence of adaptive thermogenesis** — 50 kcal/day (some analyses find it negligible after proper adjustment) to 300–500 kcal/day (Biggest Loser). Method of adjusting for body composition drives most of the disagreement.
7. **Menstrual-phase-based programming** — mechanistically plausible, empirically weak.
8. **Fasted vs. fed cardio** — no difference in 24 h fat balance or longer-term fat loss when calories are matched.
9. **The absolute "500 kcal" deficit ceiling** — useful heuristic, but body-size-blind; % of TDEE is the more defensible framing.
10. **Whether recomposition is possible in advanced trainees** — case reports exist; group means say essentially no.
