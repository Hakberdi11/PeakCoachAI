# Evidence-Graded Review: Maximizing Skeletal Muscle Hypertrophy

**Confidence key:** High = multiple meta-analyses/systematic reviews with consistent direction. Moderate = several RCTs or a single meta-analysis, some heterogeneity. Low = mechanistic reasoning, single trials, expert consensus, or contested data.

---

## 1. Training Volume

### 1.1 Core dose-response

| Metric | Number | Confidence | Evidence type |
|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum effective volume (MEV) for measurable growth | **~4 hard sets/muscle/week** (some growth as low as 2–3) | Moderate | Dose-response meta-regressions; low-volume RCTs |
| Maintenance volume (MV) | **2–6 sets/muscle/week**, or 1/3 to 1/9 of accumulation volume, holds mass 8–32 weeks | Moderate | Bickel 2011 detraining/maintenance RCT + reviews |
| Volume where ~80–90% of achievable growth occurs | **10–20 hard sets/muscle/week** | High | Multiple hypertrophy meta-analyses |
| Marginal return per added set | **~0.24% additional hypertrophy per set** at a baseline of ~12 fractional sets/week | High | Pelland et al. 2025 meta-regression (67 studies, n=2058) |
| Upper bound where the curve is still (weakly) positive | **~30–40 sets/week**, with wide credible intervals and steeply diminishing returns | Low–Moderate | Same meta-regression; sparse data above 30 sets |
| Volume needed for **strength** (as opposed to size) | Plateaus at **~2–5 direct sets/muscle/week**; near-max at ~5–10 | High | Same meta-regression + strength volume meta-analyses |

Key interpretive point: hypertrophy is **dose-dependent with diminishing returns and no clearly identified hard ceiling in the literature**, but the practical ceiling is set by recovery, time, and injury risk, not by the dose-response curve. The classic tiered finding (<5 sets/wk ES ≈ 0.24; 5–9 sets ES ≈ 0.34; 10+ sets ES ≈ 0.44) remains directionally intact. (High)

### 1.2 By training status

| Status | Weekly sets per muscle | MEV | Productive range | Practical ceiling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Untrained / novice (0–6 mo) | **6–10** | ~3–4 | 6–12 | ~15 (extra volume adds little; adds soreness) |
| Novice–intermediate (6–24 mo) | **10–16** | ~5–6 | 10–18 | ~20–22 |
| Intermediate (2–5 yr) | **12–20** | ~6–8 | 12–22 | ~25 |
| Advanced (5+ yr) | **15–25** | ~8–10 | 15–25 | **~25–35** for lagging muscles, in time-limited blocks of 4–8 weeks |

Confidence: **Moderate.** Meta-analyses are dominated by untrained/lightly trained subjects (~80% male, mean age ~25). The advanced-lifter numbers are extrapolation plus coaching consensus. (Low for the >25-set figures specifically.)

### 1.3 Maximum recoverable volume (MRV)

"MRV" is a **useful heuristic without direct validation** — no study has measured an individual MRV prospectively. (Low confidence in the construct's precision; Moderate confidence that per-individual recovery ceilings exist.)

Operational proxies rather than a fixed number:
- Reps at a fixed load drop **>5%** across two consecutive sessions for the same muscle.
- Session RPE creeps **≥1 point** for identical prescribed work over 2 weeks.
- Joint/tendon discomfort persists **>72 h**.
- Practical guideline: hold weekly sets per muscle at **≤ 2× your MEV in a first block**, then ramp **+1–2 sets/muscle/week** across a 4–6 week accumulation block.

### 1.4 Counting rules
- Count only sets taken to **≤4 RIR** ("hard sets"). (Moderate)
- Indirect/secondary involvement counts as **0.5 sets** (e.g., biceps from rows, triceps from presses, hamstrings from RDLs when the target is quads). This "fractional" scheme is what the 2025 dose-response meta-regression used. (Moderate)

## 2. Intensity (Load, %1RM, RIR)

### 2.1 Load

| Goal | %1RM | Reps | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy — primary zone | **65–80% 1RM** | **6–12** | High |
| Hypertrophy — full effective range | **30–85% 1RM** | **5–30** | High |
| Hypertrophy — heavy accessory band | **80–87%** | 4–6 | Moderate |
| Strength-biased | **80–95%** | **1–6** | High |
| Muscular endurance / metabolite | **30–50%** | **15–30+** | Moderate |
| Below the effective threshold | **<30% 1RM** | >35 | Moderate (20% 1RM shown inferior even to failure) |

**Load is largely interchangeable for hypertrophy when sets are volume-equated and taken near failure** — 6–12 vs 20–30 reps produce statistically indistinguishable growth. (High) But:
- **Strength** is strongly load-dependent: heavy-load 1RM effect size ≈ **1.07** vs light-load ≈ **0.28**. (High)
- High-rep sets (>15) carry a much greater discomfort/effort cost per unit growth and higher RIR-estimation error. (Moderate)
- Very low reps (1–5) are **less time-efficient per set** for hypertrophy: you need roughly **2–3× the sets** to equate stimulus. (Moderate)

**Practical allocation:** ~**60–70% of weekly sets in 6–12 reps**, ~15–20% in 4–6 (strength/load-progression driver), ~15–20% in 12–20+ (joint-friendly, isolation, metabolite). Confidence: Low–Moderate (this specific split is consensus programming, not a trial result).

### 2.2 Proximity to failure (RIR/RPE)

| Application | Target RIR | RPE (10-pt) | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy, isolation/machine/single-joint | **0–2 RIR** | 8–10 | High |
| Hypertrophy, multi-joint free-weight compound | **1–3 RIR** | 7–9 | High |
| Strength work at ≥85% 1RM | **2–4 RIR** | 6–8 | High |
| Deload / technique work | **4–6 RIR** | 4–6 | Moderate |
| Beginners (first 3–6 mo) | **2–4 RIR** | 6–8 | Moderate |

- Hypertrophy improves continuously as mean RIR decreases from ~5 toward 0, but the **effect is small (ES ≈ 0.15–0.21 for failure vs. non-failure)** and falls off much more steeply once mean RIR exceeds ~5. (High — Refalo meta-regressions)
- Training to **momentary failure is not required**; 1–2 RIR yields statistically equivalent hypertrophy in trained individuals over 8 weeks, with lower fatigue cost. (High)
- Failure training **impairs subsequent strength expression and volume performance**; cap true-failure sets at roughly **the last set of each exercise, ≤ 20–25% of total weekly sets**. (Moderate)
- RIR estimation error in novices is **±1–3 reps** (systematically overestimating how many reps remain, i.e. stopping too early); accuracy improves to **±1 rep** after ~8–12 weeks of RIR-anchored practice and is better on isolation than compound lifts, and better at ≤4 RIR than at >4 RIR. (Moderate)

**Contested area:** the "effective reps"/stimulating-reps model (only the last ~5 reps before failure count) is mechanistically appealing but **not confirmed**; volume-load and set-count models predict outcomes about as well. Expert opinion ranges from "only reps within 5 of failure matter" to "total hard sets is the dominant variable, RIR a minor modifier." (Low)

## 3. Frequency

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sessions per muscle per week — general recommendation | **2–3×** | High |
| Frequency effect when weekly volume is equated | **No meaningful difference** between 1×, 2×, 3×, or 4× | High |
| Frequency effect when volume is NOT equated | 2×/wk > 1×/wk (ES ≈ 0.49 vs 0.30) — because 2× enables more volume | High |
| Practical per-session ceiling per muscle | **~6–10 hard sets**; returns diminish sharply beyond ~10 | Moderate |
| Total training sessions per week | **3–6** | High |
| Session duration | **45–90 min** of working time; beyond ~90 min per-set quality falls | Low–Moderate |
| Minimum spacing between sessions hitting the same muscle | **~48 h** for heavy compound work; **24 h** acceptable for lower-fatigue isolation | Moderate |

**Frequency is a logistics variable, not an independent driver.** The rule that falls out of the evidence:

- ≤10 weekly sets per muscle → **1–2 sessions**
- 11–20 weekly sets → **2–3 sessions**
- 21–30 weekly sets → **3–4 sessions**
- (Confidence: Moderate — derived from the per-session ceiling plus equated-volume frequency data.)

Split templates by weekly frequency (Moderate confidence, mostly practical):
- **3 days/wk:** full body — each muscle 3×/wk, 3–5 sets/session
- **4 days/wk:** upper/lower ×2 — each muscle 2×/wk, 5–10 sets/session
- **5 days/wk:** upper/lower/push/pull/legs or U-L-rest-U-L — 2×/wk
- **6 days/wk:** push/pull/legs ×2 — 2×/wk, best for weekly volumes >20 sets/muscle

## 4. Progressive Overload

### 4.1 Mechanisms, ranked by practical utility

| Lever | Prescription | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Load** | +2.5–5% when top of rep range is hit on all sets; upper body **+1–2.5 kg**, lower body **+2.5–5 kg** absolute jumps | High |
| **Reps (double progression)** | Work from bottom to top of a 3-rep-wide window (e.g., 8→10 or 10→12) before adding load | High |
| **Sets** | +1–2 sets/muscle/week within a 4–6 wk accumulation block; reset at deload | Moderate |
| **Range of motion** | Progress from partial to full ROM; a training-quality lever, not an infinite one | Moderate |
| **Density** | Cut rest by 10–15 s at fixed volume-load — a *conditioning* progression; **weak hypertrophy lever**, can reduce stimulus if reps fall | Low–Moderate |
| **Tempo / time under tension** | Eccentrics **2–4 s**; total rep 2–6 s. Rep durations **0.5–8 s all work**; >8 s per rep impairs growth | Moderate |
| **Exercise difficulty progression** | Regress→progress chains (e.g., incline push-up → push-up → deficit → weighted) | Moderate |

**Do not progress more than one variable at a time within a block.** (Low — consensus)

### 4.2 Realistic progression rates by training age

Strength (1RM on a trained compound lift):

| Training age | Per week | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| True beginner (0–3 mo) | **+2–5%** | +8–20% | **+40–60%** over 12 months |
| Novice (3–12 mo) | +1–2% | +4–8% | +25–40% |
| Intermediate (1–3 yr) | +0.25–0.75% | **+1–3%** | +10–20% |
| Advanced (3–5 yr) | ~+0.1% | +0.5–1% | **+5–10%** |
| Highly advanced (5+ yr) | negligible weekly | <0.5% | **+1–5%** |

Confidence: **Moderate** (beginner numbers High; advanced numbers Low–Moderate, drawn from longitudinal powerlifting/competition data rather than RCTs).

Lean mass accrual (drug-free, adequate nutrition and sleep):

| Training age | Male | Female | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | **0.7–1.1 kg/month** (~9–11 kg/yr) | **0.35–0.55 kg/month** (~4.5–5.5 kg/yr) | Moderate |
| Year 2 | 0.35–0.5 kg/month (~4–5 kg/yr) | 0.2–0.25 kg/month (~2–2.5 kg/yr) | Moderate |
| Year 3 | 0.2–0.25 kg/month (~2–2.5 kg/yr) | 0.1–0.13 kg/month (~1–1.5 kg/yr) | Low–Moderate |
| Year 4+ | **0.05–0.15 kg/month** (~1–2 kg/yr) | 0.03–0.08 kg/month (~0.5–1 kg/yr) | Low |

These halving-per-year models (Aragon/McDonald-type heuristics) are **coaching frameworks calibrated against longitudinal data, not meta-analytic outputs**. Treat as order-of-magnitude. Individual response variance is large: in 8–12 week RCTs, hypertrophy responses range from **~0% to ~15%** cross-sectional area change on identical programs, with roughly **5–15% of participants classified as low/non-responders** at short timescales. (Moderate)

### 4.3 Periodization

| Model | Best fit | Effect size vs. non-periodized | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear progression (session-to-session) | Novice, 0–9 months | Large in this population | High |
| Daily/weekly undulating (DUP) | Intermediate | ≈ equal to linear for hypertrophy; small edge (**ES ~0.1–0.3**) for strength | Moderate |
| Block / phase potentiation | Advanced | Small edge for strength peaking; **no clear hypertrophy advantage** | Low–Moderate |
| No formal periodization but progressive overload | All | Only modestly worse | Moderate |

**Bottom line:** periodization confers a **small (ES ~0.2–0.3) advantage for strength** and **no reliable advantage for hypertrophy** over simply progressing load/reps. Its main value is fatigue management. (Moderate)

## 5. Rest Periods

| Context | Seconds | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertrophy — multi-joint compound (squat, deadlift, press, row) | **120–300 s** (target 150–180) | Moderate–High |
| Hypertrophy — single-joint / machine isolation | **60–120 s** | Moderate |
| Hypertrophy — absolute floor | **>60 s**; <60 s shows a small but consistent decrement | High (Bayesian meta-analysis, 2024) |
| Beyond 90 s | Additional benefit **unclear/small** — data thin | Moderate |
| Maximal strength | **180–300 s** | High |
| Power / speed work | **120–300 s** | Moderate |
| Metabolite-focused techniques (drop sets, myo-reps, rest-pause) | **15–30 s** intra-set, applied to **≤10–20% of weekly sets** | Moderate |
| Antagonist paired sets / non-competing supersets | **60–90 s** between pairs; preserves performance, cuts session time **~30–50%** | Moderate |

Notes:
- The mechanism is straightforward: short rest reduces reps performed on subsequent sets, which reduces volume-load. **If you can hold reps constant with shorter rest, shorter rest is not harmful.** (High)
- Reps lost across sets with 1 min vs 2–3 min rest is where the largest performance gap appears; the 2 min → 3 min gap is much smaller. (Moderate)
- Practical rule: rest **until you can complete the prescribed reps at the prescribed RIR**, using the table as a starting bracket. (Moderate)
- Short rest does **not** meaningfully increase hypertrophy via "metabolic stress" as once claimed. (High — this was the contested position and it has resolved against short rest.)

## 6. Exercise Selection

### 6.1 Compound vs. isolation

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Exercises per session | **4–8** | Low–Moderate |
| Compounds per session | **1–3** (first, when fresh) | Moderate |
| Isolation exercises per session | **2–5** | Moderate |
| Distinct exercises per muscle per week | **2–4** | Low–Moderate |
| Weekly set split | ~**50–70% compound / 30–50% isolation** | Low |

- Compounds deliver more total muscle stimulated per unit time; isolation is **required** for muscles with poor indirect coverage — **biceps, triceps long head, lateral/rear delts, calves, hamstrings (knee flexion), forearms, abdominals, neck**. (Moderate)
- Adding direct arm work to a compound-only program produces **additional ~5–10% arm hypertrophy** over 8–12 weeks. (Moderate)
- **Machines vs. free weights: equivalent hypertrophy** when effort and volume are matched. Machines allow closer-to-failure training with lower stabilizer fatigue and lower injury risk. (High)

### 6.2 Range of motion and stretch-mediated hypertrophy

| Finding | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Full ROM vs. short-length partials | Full ROM superior, **ES ≈ 0.2–0.4** | High |
| Lengthened (stretched-position) partials vs. full ROM | **Equivalent or slightly favoring lengthened partials** | Moderate (2023 meta favored partials at long lengths; 2025 direct trials found equivalence) |
| Training at long vs. short muscle lengths | Long-length advantage of **~0–50%** more growth depending on muscle and protocol; median effect much closer to ~10–25% | Moderate |
| Passive/loaded stretching as a standalone stimulus | Small but real hypertrophy effect; requires **long durations (>5 min per set, >15 min/wk per muscle)** — poor time efficiency | Moderate |
| Practical prescription | **≥1 exercise per muscle per week emphasizing the lengthened position**; use full ROM as default | Moderate |

Concrete long-length exercise picks (Moderate confidence, regional-hypertrophy trials):
- **Triceps:** overhead extensions > pushdowns for long head (reported advantages of ~30–40% in some trials)
- **Hamstrings:** seated leg curl > lying leg curl; RDL for hip-hinge component
- **Quads:** deep squats / leg press with full knee flexion > partial; **sissy squat / leg extension with hip extended** for rectus femoris
- **Calves:** seated calf raise (soleus) + standing (gastroc), full dorsiflexion
- **Biceps:** incline dumbbell curl (shoulder extended) > preacher curl for long-head length
- **Pecs:** deep flyes / cable crossovers at long length + press
- **Lats:** full-stretch pullover / straight-arm pulldown + vertical pull
- **Glutes:** deficit/ATG split squats, RDL, hip thrust for short-length peak contraction (complementary, not a substitute)

### 6.3 Unilateral vs. bilateral

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Bilateral deficit (sum of unilateral force > bilateral force) | **5–25%** | High |
| Hypertrophy difference, unilateral vs. bilateral, volume-equated | **None detected** | Moderate |
| Cross-education (contralateral strength gain from training one limb) | **~7–12%** strength gain in untrained limb; minimal hypertrophy | Moderate |
| Time cost of unilateral work | **~1.6–2× session time** for equivalent sets | High (arithmetic) |

Use unilateral work when: limb asymmetry **>10–15%** in strength or size; spinal loading must be limited; balance/stability is a goal; or during rehab of one side. (Moderate)

### 6.4 Exercise variation

- **2–3 exercise variations per muscle per training block** appears optimal; more variation can improve regional/complete development but **excessive rotation (changing exercises every session) impairs load progression and skill acquisition**. (Low–Moderate)
- Hold core exercises constant for **4–8 weeks** minimum before rotating. (Low)

## 7. Recovery Between Sessions

### 7.1 Time-course

| Process | Time course | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| MPS elevation after resistance exercise — **untrained** | Peaks 4–24 h, elevated **up to 48–72 h** | High |
| MPS elevation — **trained** | Peaks 2–8 h, largely returned to baseline by **~24–36 h** | High |
| Practical implication | Trained lifters benefit from **≥2×/wk per muscle** because the MPS window closes faster | Moderate |
| DOMS onset / peak / resolution | Onset 6–12 h, peak **24–48 h**, resolved **72–96 h** | High |
| DOMS after novel/eccentric-emphasis or very high volume | **5–7 days** | High |
| Force recovery after a typical hypertrophy session | **24–48 h** (upper body faster) | Moderate |
| Force recovery after high-eccentric/high-volume lower body | **48–96 h** (hamstrings and adductors slowest) | Moderate |
| Repeated-bout effect (soreness reduction after 1st exposure) | **~50–80% reduction**, protective for **4–8 weeks** | High |

Muscle-specific recovery rank (fastest → slowest), Low–Moderate confidence: **calves/forearms/delts < biceps/triceps < chest/back < quads < hamstrings/glutes/lower back/spinal erectors**.

**Important:** DOMS is a **poor proxy for recovery or for growth**. Soreness correlates weakly with hypertrophy outcomes; do not program to chase it. (High)

### 7.2 Autoregulation markers

| Marker | Under-recovery threshold | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Reps at a fixed load (rep-based autoregulation) | **↓ >5%** (or ≥1 rep on a set of ~10) vs. last session, on two consecutive exposures | Moderate |
| Mean concentric bar velocity at a fixed load | **↓ >10%** vs. baseline for that load | Moderate |
| Session RPE at identical prescribed work | **↑ ≥1 point** sustained over 2 weeks | Moderate |
| Countermovement jump height | **↓ >5–10%** vs. 7-day rolling average | Moderate |
| Grip strength | **↓ >5%** vs. baseline | Low–Moderate |
| Resting HR | **↑ >5–7 bpm** vs. 7-day average | Low–Moderate |
| HRV (rMSSD, 7-day rolling avg) | **↓ >0.5–1.0 SD** below individual baseline for 3+ days | **Low** for resistance training specifically — HRV is validated far better for endurance than for strength/hypertrophy |
| Subjective wellness (sleep, mood, soreness, stress; 1–5 scales) | Composite drop **>10–15%** vs. baseline | Moderate — subjective measures often outperform objective ones |
| Body mass | Unintentional **↓ >2%** over 1 week | Moderate |

**RPE/RIR autoregulation prescription:** set an RPE target per set (e.g., "3×6 @ RPE 8"), let the lifter select load, and adjust load between sets by **±2.5–5%** when actual RPE deviates from target by ≥1 point. This yields equal-or-better outcomes than fixed percentage prescription. (Moderate)

### 7.3 Deloads

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency — scheduled | Every **4–8 weeks** (novices can often go 8–12+ weeks) | Low–Moderate |
| Frequency — autoregulated (preferred) | When 2+ under-recovery markers trip, or performance stalls for 2 consecutive sessions | Moderate |
| Duration | **5–7 days** (one microcycle) | Low–Moderate |
| Volume reduction | **↓ 30–50%** of sets | Low–Moderate |
| Intensity/load reduction | **↓ 0–20%** of load; keep at least ~80–90% of load, drop volume preferentially | Low–Moderate |
| Proximity to failure during deload | **4–6 RIR** | Low |
| Expected performance restoration | 3–7 days | Low |

**This is the weakest-evidenced section of the report.** Direct RCT evidence that scheduled deloads improve long-term hypertrophy is scarce, and at least one review concluded planned deloads may **cost** volume without demonstrable benefit in non-elite lifters. Expert opinion ranges from "mandatory every 4–6 weeks" (high-volume bodybuilding coaches) to "unnecessary if you autoregulate volume and never systematically overreach" (some evidence-based coaches). **Confidence: Low.** Reasonable default: autoregulated deloads, not calendar-driven.

## 8. Sleep

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Target sleep duration, general adult | **7–9 h/night** | High |
| Target for hard-training lifters | **7–9 h**, with **8–10 h** during high-volume blocks or heavy caloric deficit | Moderate |
| Sleep consistency (bed/wake time variance) | **±30–60 min** | Moderate |
| Sleep efficiency target | **>85%** | Moderate |
| One night of total sleep deprivation → MPS | **−18%** | Moderate (single controlled trial) |
| One night of total sleep deprivation → testosterone | **−24%** | Moderate |
| One night of total sleep deprivation → cortisol | **+21%** | Moderate |
| 5 h/night × 1 week → daytime testosterone (young men) | **−10–15%** | Moderate |
| 5.5 h vs. 8.5 h/night during a caloric deficit | **~60% more lean mass lost, ~55% less fat lost** for identical calorie deficit | Moderate (Nedeltcheva 2010, small n) |
| Sleep restriction → maximal strength (compound lifts) | **−2–10%** acute decrement; largely rescued by caffeine/motivation | Moderate |
| Sleep restriction → single-joint/isometric force | Often **unaffected** — the deficit is largely central/motivational | Moderate |
| **<8 h/night** in athletes → injury risk | **~1.7× higher** injury odds | Moderate (adolescent athlete cohort; generalization to adults is Low) |
| Chronic <6 h/night | Associated with reduced training volume tolerance, elevated perceived exertion, impaired glucose tolerance | Moderate |
| Sleep extension (+1–2 h/night for 5–7 wk) | Improves sprint, reaction time, accuracy by **3–10%** | Moderate |
| Nap prescription | **20–30 min** (avoid 30–90 min, sleep inertia), before 15:00 | Moderate |
| Post-lunch nap of 60–90 min after poor night | Restores ~50% of performance decrement | Low–Moderate |
| Pre-sleep protein | **30–40 g casein**, 30 min before bed → increases overnight MPS **~20–22%** | Moderate |
| Caffeine cutoff before bed | **≥8–9 h** (caffeine half-life 5–6 h) | Moderate |
| Alcohol | **>0.5 g/kg** near bedtime suppresses MPS **~24–37%** post-exercise and degrades REM | Moderate |
| Room temperature | **17–19 °C** | Low–Moderate |

**Framing:** sleep is best understood as a **permissive** variable — adequate sleep does not accelerate hypertrophy above normal, but restriction below ~6 h imposes a measurable penalty on strength expression, training volume tolerance, and lean-mass retention during deficits. (Moderate)

## 9. Nutrition

### 9.1 Protein — total daily

| Population / context | g/kg bodyweight/day | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Meta-analytic breakpoint for added hypertrophy benefit | **1.62 g/kg** (95% CI extending to ~2.2) | High (Morton 2018, 49 RCTs) |
| Alternative dose-response estimate | Slope inflection ~**1.3 g/kg**, benefit continuing to ~1.6 | High (Tagawa 2020, 105 studies) |
| Maintenance or caloric surplus, trained lifter | **1.6–2.2 g/kg** | High |
| Caloric deficit, lean lifter | **2.3–3.1 g/kg fat-free mass** ≈ **2.0–2.7 g/kg BW** for a lean individual | Moderate (Helms 2014 review) |
| Aggressive deficit (>25%) or very lean (<10% BF male) | Upper end: **2.7–3.1 g/kg BW** | Moderate |
| Obese individuals (BMI >30) | **1.2–1.5 g/kg total BW**, or 1.6–2.2 g/kg of *ideal/fat-free* mass | Moderate |
| Adults **>50–60 yr** | **1.2–1.6 g/kg minimum**; **1.6–2.2 g/kg** if resistance training | High–Moderate |
| Adults **>70 yr** | **1.2–2.0 g/kg**, upper end if training | Moderate |
| Vegan/plant-based | **+20–40%** above the above ranges (lower leucine density, lower digestibility) | Moderate |
| Adolescents (14–18) | **1.6–2.0 g/kg** | Low–Moderate |
| Point of no further benefit | **>2.2–2.5 g/kg** shows no additional hypertrophy in surplus/maintenance | High |
| Upper safety bound in healthy kidneys | Intakes to **3.3 g/kg** for 8+ weeks show no adverse renal/hepatic markers | Moderate |

### 9.2 Protein distribution and meal frequency

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Meals per day (protein-containing) | **3–6** | Moderate |
| Protein per meal, young adults | **0.24–0.40 g/kg** (≈ 20–40 g) | High |
| Protein per meal, adults >60 (anabolic resistance) | **0.40 g/kg** (≈ 30–40 g) | High |
| Interval between protein feedings | **3–5 h** | Moderate |
| Leucine threshold per meal, young | **2.0–3.0 g** | Moderate |
| Leucine threshold per meal, older | **3.0–4.0 g** | Moderate |
| Independent effect of even distribution vs. skewed, once total is adequate | **Small to negligible** in meta-analysis | Moderate |
| Ceiling on single-meal anabolic response | Not a hard ceiling — a 100 g bolus prolongs MPS for **>12 h**; the "30 g maximum" claim is **refuted** | Moderate (recent tracer work) |

**Interpretation:** total daily protein is the dominant variable by a wide margin. Distribution is a **second-order optimization worth perhaps 0–5%** of the outcome. (Moderate)

### 9.3 Energy balance

**Surplus (muscle gain):**

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Surplus size | **+10–20% above TDEE**, ≈ **+200–500 kcal/day** | Moderate |
| Novice / underweight surplus | **+15–25%**, up to +500–700 kcal/day | Moderate |
| Advanced lifter surplus | **+5–10%**, ≈ +100–250 kcal/day | Low–Moderate |
| Target rate of weight gain — novice | **0.25–0.5% BW/week** | Moderate |
| Target rate — intermediate | **0.25–0.35% BW/week** | Moderate |
| Target rate — advanced | **0.1–0.25% BW/week** | Low–Moderate |
| Rate above which gain is predominantly fat | **>0.5–1.0% BW/week** | Moderate (elite-athlete trials: large surplus → ~5× the fat gain, no extra lean mass) |
| Body-fat ceiling before switching to a cut (male / female) | **~15–20% / ~25–30%** | Low (largely consensus; p-ratio worsens with adiposity) |
| Energy cost per kg of lean mass gained | **~5,000–8,000 kcal/kg** | Low–Moderate |

## 10. Individual Differences

### 10.1 Sex

| Parameter | Finding | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Relative** hypertrophy (% change from baseline) | **No significant sex difference** | High (Bayesian meta-analysis 2025, plus earlier meta-analyses) |
| **Absolute** hypertrophy (cm²/kg) | Slightly favors males, driven by larger starting muscle mass | High |
| Relative **upper-body strength** gains | Slightly **favor females** (ES ~0.6 in one meta-analysis) | Moderate |
| Relative **lower-body strength** gains | No significant difference | Moderate |
| **Fatigue resistance at submaximal loads** | Females perform **~1–3 more reps** at a given %1RM and sustain reps across sets far better — in one protocol, females completed **~2× total reps** across a multi-set fatigue protocol at 75% 1RM with 90 s rest | Moderate–High |
| Mechanism | Primarily **faster recovery between sets** (greater type I fiber proportional area, better muscle perfusion, lower absolute force output), not slower within-set fatigue | Moderate |
| Between-**session** recovery | Appears **similar** between sexes despite higher relative workload completed by females; some evidence of faster female recovery from eccentric damage | Moderate |
| **Volume tolerance** | Plausibly higher for females at matched relative intensity; **no direct trial has established a different optimal weekly set number by sex** | **Low** |
| Programming adjustments justified by evidence | (a) Rest periods can be **~20–30% shorter** for females at matched %1RM; (b) females may tolerate **slightly higher reps at a given %1RM** — so a given %1RM maps to a different RIR (e.g., 80% 1RM ≈ 8–10 reps for many women vs. 6–8 for many men); (c) potentially **+10–20% weekly sets** if recovery markers permit | Low–Moderate |

**Commonly claimed sex differences that are weakly supported or unsupported — state explicitly:**
- "Women should train higher reps / lighter loads." **Not supported.** Women respond to heavy loading equivalently; the load-hypertrophy relationship is sex-neutral. (High confidence in the refutation.)
- "Women need lower volume." **Not supported**; if anything the evidence leans the other way. (Moderate)
- "Menstrual-cycle-based (follicular-phase-emphasized) periodization improves hypertrophy." **Weakly supported.** Meta-analytic effects of cycle phase on strength performance are **trivial (ES ~0.06)** with very low-quality evidence. Some individual-level symptom-driven autoregulation is defensible; systematic phase-based volume loading is not evidence-backed. Confidence: **Low**, and this is genuinely contested — expert opinion ranges from "phase-based programming is a meaningful lever" to "the effect is smaller than day-to-day noise."
- "Hormonal contraceptives blunt hypertrophy." **Not supported** — meta-analyses show negligible differences in lean mass gains between users and non-users. (Moderate)
- "The acute testosterone/GH spike after training drives female (or male) hypertrophy." **Refuted** — acute post-exercise hormone elevations do not predict hypertrophy in either sex. (High)
- Females may have **higher relative gains in the upper body** simply due to a lower trained baseline; this is a starting-point artifact, not a physiological superiority. (Moderate)

## 11. Injuries and Limitations — Training-Science Principles

*(General training-science principles only; not medical advice. Anyone with pain, a diagnosed injury, or a medical condition should be evaluated by a qualified clinician.)*

### 11.1 Pain-monitoring framework

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Acceptable pain during loading (0–10 NRS) | **≤3/10**, and "acceptable" not "unacceptable" to the individual | Moderate (pain-monitoring model, tendinopathy literature) |
| Pain must return to baseline by | **24 h post-session** | Moderate |
| Pain must not increase week over week | Across a **7-day** rolling window | Moderate |
| If any criterion is violated | Reduce load **20–50%**, or regress one step in the exercise chain | Low–Moderate |

### 11.2 Regression/substitution hierarchy

Apply in this order, changing **one variable at a time** (Low–Moderate confidence; consensus rehab-programming logic):

1. **Reduce load** 20–50%, hold ROM, reps, and exercise constant.
2. **Restrict ROM** to the pain-free arc; re-expand by **10–20° per week** as tolerated.
3. **Change tempo** — slow eccentric (3–6 s) reduces peak force demand at a given load; or remove the eccentric entirely (concentric-only) for irritable tissue.
4. **Change implement** — barbell → dumbbell → cable → machine (each step reduces stability demand and gives more joint-angle freedom).
5. **Change to unilateral** — halves spinal/systemic load and lets the healthy side keep training.
6. **Change the movement pattern** — e.g., barbell back squat → hack squat / leg press / belt squat / split squat, all of which unload the spine.
7. **Change to isolation** — remove the painful joint from the chain (e.g., painful shoulder in bench press → machine chest press with neutral grip, or pec deck).
8. **Train the contralateral limb** — cross-education preserves **~7–12%** of strength in an immobilized limb. (Moderate)
9. **Blood flow restriction** where heavy loading is contraindicated: **20–30% 1RM**, cuff at **40–50% AOP (upper limb) / 60–80% AOP (lower limb)**, **30-15-15-15 reps**, **30 s rest**, 2–3×/wk. Produces hypertrophy comparable to heavy training in load-restricted populations. (Moderate–High)

### 11.3 Region-specific principles

| Limitation | Prescription | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Tendinopathy (patellar, Achilles, elbow, rotator cuff)** | Isometrics **5 × 45 s at 70% MVIC**, 2 min rest, for acute analgesia; then heavy slow resistance **3–4 sets × 6–15 reps at 3 s up / 3 s down, 3×/wk for 12 weeks** | Moderate–High |
| **Low back pain** | Do not avoid loading; **reduce spinal compressive load** (machines, supported/chest-supported rows, belt/hack squat, hip thrust), keep training frequency; graded exposure back to hinging | Moderate |
| **Shoulder impingement-type pain** | Neutral/semi-supinated grips, reduce overhead ROM to below the painful arc, favor **landmine press / incline press ≤30–45°**, add external rotation and lower-trap work **2 × 10–20 reps, 2–3×/wk** | Low–Moderate |
| **Knee pain (patellofemoral)** | Restrict knee flexion depth to pain-free, favor **hip-dominant** work (RDL, hip thrust, back extension), reintroduce depth **+10–20°/wk**; quad strengthening is protective long-term, not causative | Moderate |
| **Post-immobilization / return from layoff** | Restart at **40–60% of pre-layoff volume and 70–80% of pre-layoff load**; expect full restoration in **1/3 to 1/2 the layoff duration** due to muscle memory (myonuclear retention) | Moderate |
| **General return-to-load progression** | **≤10% weekly increase** in load or volume | Low–Moderate |
| **Detraining timeline** | Strength largely preserved **2–4 weeks**; measurable atrophy from **~3 weeks**; ~**5–10%** cross-sectional area loss by 8–12 weeks fully untrained. **1 session/week at 1/3 volume** maintains mass for months | Moderate |

### 11.4 General injury-risk baseline

| Parameter | Number | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance training injury incidence | **~1–4 injuries per 1,000 h** of training — lower than most sports | High |
| Highest-risk contexts | Maximal (1RM) attempts, rapid load progression (>10%/wk), technical failure under fatigue, sleep <6 h | Moderate |
| Warm-up | **5–10 min general** + **2–4 ramp sets** per first exercise of a pattern; reduces injury risk and improves first-set performance | Moderate |
| Static stretching immediately pre-lifting | Avoid holds **>60 s**; acute strength decrement **~3–5%**. Short holds (<30 s) are harmless | High |

## 12. How Users Actually Perceive/Notice Improvement

The scale and the 1RM are necessary tracking tools but a poor match for how hypertrophy progress *feels* day to day. Programming a coach around the wrong signal (especially bodyweight) sets users up to feel like they're failing when they're not, and is a well-documented driver of adherence dropout. This section covers what to track and surface, and on what timescale results actually become noticeable.

| Signal | Timescale to notice | Reliability as a hypertrophy proxy | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bodyweight scale | Daily fluctuation of **±0.5–2 kg** from water/glycogen/food/sodium/GI content alone | **Poor** for hypertrophy specifically — a lean bulk can show flat or even declining scale weight for weeks while lean mass is accruing, and a recomp can hold scale weight constant for months | High (that it's a poor/noisy proxy) |
| Rep PRs at fixed weight (double progression) | **Session-to-session, weekly** | **High** — the most immediate, least noisy signal available; directly reflects the mechanism (progressive overload) driving the growth | High |
| Estimated 1RM / working-weight trend | **2–4 weeks** for a clearly visible upward trend | High — but conflates early neural adaptation with hypertrophy in novices (first 4–8 weeks); more purely reflects hypertrophy after ~8–12 weeks of training age | Moderate–High |
| Circumference measurements (arm, thigh, chest, waist) | **First measurable change: 4–8 weeks**; clearly noticeable: **8–12 weeks** | Moderate — measurement error (±0.5–1 cm from tape placement/tension/time-of-day) can swamp true signal in the first month; needs a consistent protocol (same tape tension, same time of day, same landmark, ideally same measurer) to be useful | Moderate |
| Progress photos | **First visible change: 6–8 weeks**; clearly visible: **12–16 weeks** | Moderate–High as a *qualitative* signal, if conditions are held constant — same lighting, pose, time of day (post-waking, pre-meal), camera distance/lens, and minimal pump difference (don't compare a post-workout pump photo to a rested one) | Moderate |
| Progress-photo cadence | **Every 2–4 weeks** — weekly is too noisy to show real change and invites over-checking; monthly+ risks missing the trend and losing motivation between checkpoints | Low–Moderate (practical consensus, not trial-derived) |
| Visible/felt muscle growth by region | Distal/smaller muscle groups (delts, arms, calves) often show visible change **before** larger groups (back, quads) at matched relative growth, because a given % cross-sectional-area increase is a bigger fraction of a smaller starting circumference | Low–Moderate (anatomical/geometric reasoning, not directly trial-measured) |
| DEXA / BIA / 3D body scanners | Vary — BIA day-to-day noise is **often larger than the true monthly signal**; DEXA is far more reliable but rarely available at consumer cadence | Low confidence in BIA; Moderate–High confidence in DEXA if accessible |
| Perceived pump/fullness during training | Same-session only — **not a valid indicator of long-term hypertrophy trend**, though a persistent *loss* of usual pump can be one input into an under-recovery/deload decision | Low (as a growth signal); Moderate (as a fatigue-monitoring input, see §7.2) |
| Clothing fit (e.g., sleeve/shoulder tightness) | **4–8 weeks** for a first noticeable change | Low–Moderate (anecdotal but directionally consistent with circumference timelines) |
| Time-to-first-visible-hypertrophy overall | Novices: measurable via imaging **as early as 3–4 weeks** (fascicle/CSA changes precede visible size); **subjectively visible to the individual: ~6–8 weeks**; **visible to others: ~8–12 weeks** | Moderate (muscle biopsy/ultrasound hypertrophy timeline studies) |

**Practical implications for what to surface to a user:**
- **Do not lead with bodyweight** as a hypertrophy progress signal; if tracked at all, frame it as a trend over **2–4 week rolling averages**, never single readings, and pair it explicitly with the caveat that flat/noisy scale weight during a hypertrophy phase is expected and not a failure signal. (High)
- **Rep/weight PRs are the fastest-arriving, lowest-noise signal** and are already being logged via normal set tracking — this is the single best "you're making progress" signal to surface early and often, since it needs no separate measurement protocol and updates every session. (High)
- **Circumference + photos are the right signals for the "am I visibly changing" question**, but only on a **4-week-minimum cadence** with a standardized protocol; surfacing them weekly will mostly show noise and can be *demotivating* rather than encouraging. (Moderate)
- **Expectation-setting matters as much as the metric**: users who are told "visible change takes ~8–12 weeks, but your strength numbers will move within 2" up front show better adherence through the early low-visible-feedback period than users left to infer this themselves — this is a behavior-science inference from the training-adherence literature, not a hypertrophy-specific trial finding. (Low–Moderate)

### The five variables that actually move the needle, in order
1. **Total hard sets per muscle per week** (10–20 for most) — the dominant training variable. (High)
2. **Proximity to failure** (0–3 RIR) — makes each set count. (High)
3. **Progressive overload over months** — load or reps must trend up. (High)
4. **Total daily protein (1.6–2.2 g/kg) and appropriate energy balance.** (High)
5. **Sleep ≥7 h and enough recovery to keep performing.** (Moderate)

Everything else in this report — frequency, rest length, exercise ordering, tempo, timing, distribution, periodization model — is **second-order optimization worth perhaps 5–20% of the total outcome combined.**
